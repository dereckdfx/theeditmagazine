Travel

Musician (and avid golf fan) Tom Mcfarland goes in search of a course less trodden

Having taken a swing on six continents, golfer and Jungle musician Tom Mcfarland was excited to go for a putter around Dunas golf course at Terras da Comporta

By Tom Mcfarland · July 15, 2026

It is summer – and the golf season is in full swing. For British golfers, it also means that we can pack away our thermal layers and look forward to those long, hazy evenings walking firm fairways in the endless search for that perfect swing. Luckily, my day job as one third of the band Jungle means I get to travel for a living. It also tends to leave me with a lot of time on my hands – long days waiting for soundcheck, or time off between gigs. Taking my clubs on tour gives me valuable time away from the chaos of living and working in very close proximity to 20 or so other people for months on end. And it gives me a person a reason to actively explore new places, meet new people, and most importantly, get into the great outdoors. But what makes a good golf course?

There are a number of factors that define how good a course is. Design and history, condition and accessibility are probably my key metrics, but I also understand the value of a very good sausage roll at the halfway house. I've begun to see how different courses can be visceral; a truly great golf course will linger in your heart for years after you've visited it.

I've been fortunate enough to have played golf on every continent except Antarctica, and have had the pleasure of playing courses both private and public. A few personal highlights so far: the Old Course at St. Andrews, the oldest golf course in the world and a site of pilgrimage for golfers across the world; Royal Melbourne Golf Club, the most famous of the courses in the city's renowned Sandbelt; and The Bluffs in Ho Tram, Vietnam, a glorious rollercoaster ride of a course designed by the legendary Australian golfer Greg Norman.

One of the most exciting new courses to catch my eye in recent years is the Dunas golf course at Terras da Comporta, opened in 2023, which was designed by David McLay Kidd, whose credentials are some of the finest in modern golf architecture. His first major project was Brandon Dunes in Oregon, a course that has sat comfortably in most global top 100 lists since it opened in 1999. Closer to home, he co-designed with American golfing legend Tom Watson the course at Beaverbrook Golf Club in Surrey, which, a decade after opening, remains one of the most glamorous.

Golf was born in Scotland. The glamour soon followed at sumptuous hotels well above par

There is nothing like taking a golfing holiday, or rather plotting your travels around seeking out the perfect course. Whether you like them or not, there are several top 100 lists published yearly. Browsing one such list, I came across the relatively new course in Comporta, an enclave of Portugal on the Alentejo coast known for its rustic European charm.

The Terras da Comporta development also boasts a course designed by Masters winner and Ryder Cup veteran Sergio Garcia, and it is set to have 250 villas and two five-star hotels. Its Dunas course is regarded as the number one course in Portugal and has been ranked the sixth best in continental Europe. It struck me, too, that Comporta would be a lovely base to explore the coast and countryside – and to experience one of the most refined ‘design’ hotels in Portugal.

Deciding where to stay while I was in the Comporta area was a no-brainer. Unlike the Algarve, Comporta is the antithesis of a big golf resort. Which can only be a good thing. If I was going sample one one of Europe's most chic and talked-about new golf courses, then what better place to head than Vermelho in Melides? Listening to my favourite golf podcasts (Chasing Scratch and The Fried Egg), I drove to the straightforward hour-and-a-half route from Lisbon.

Melides appears at first glance to be a quaint if unassuming village. However, if you wriggle your way through its labyrinthine cobbled streets (and roadworks), you will find one of Portugal's most beautiful hotels. Vermelho is the brainchild of both the region's mayor and Christian Louboutin – it came about after the shoe designer moved here and wasn't able to find a dinner spot he wanted to return to every night. He decided to build himself a restaurant, only to be persuaded by the mayor that a hotel was a much better idea – and thank goodness for that. The result is an artistic homage to the area that feels both rustic and elegant, but without any sense of formality. The fact that there are only 13 rooms, each totally unique, makes it feel as though you have the place to yourself. Even so, the exclusivity has a friendly quality. That's especially true of the bedrooms, a particular jewel being one bright with red and yellow tiling, where decorative woven screens in matching tones hide a minibar, coffee machine and other such unsightly, if necessary, modernities. The bathroom is tiled in a crimson that envelops you as you enter it; rococo mosaics line the enormous bath; and even the curlicue hinges on on the red-painted shutters were custom made for the property.

You may well question whether they truly only began building the hotel in 2017, such is the romantic patina. But if it feels as though it has existed for centuries, that's not because it's scruffy or tired, but because it feels so embedded into Melides and the surrounding landscape. The whole place is profoundly restorative, especially after a deep-tissue massage from Tim, who has surprisingly soft hands for such a big man. But you can also find peace when you simply lie by the lovely little pool, surrounded by marble statues and pomegranate trees, and order a chilled glass of local wine. Meanwhile, the restaurant, Xtian, has an exciting and well-executed menu of local dishes – such as cod pil pil – alongside some unique takes on European classics (the gazpacho is out of this world). The hotel's manager, Vera Diana Gonçalves, is also brilliant and a golfer's dream, helping to arrange my time at Dunas with such ease and charm.

Very early the next morning, it was time to tear myself away from Vermelho and drive about 25 minutes north to the next golf course. One of the most exciting parts of going to a new course is the first glimpse you get of a green or a fairway. Some courses, like St. Andrews, are laid out in front of you from the moment you get there, but Dunas holds on to her secrets almost until you're on the first tee box. The tantalising drive from the main road snakes through towering pines and rugged dunes until you reach a car park where you are given your buggy and shown the way to the pro-shop . I had the first tee time; the morning mist still clung to the air and soaked the grass with dew as I took my first swing.

The course was beguiling. Hole after hole, I was escorted through the pine forest on a carpet of lush green grass. There had been a good amount of rain but the course was still firm and fast, a testament to both agronomy and the sand-base that the course is built on. The sweeping elevation changes are what defined Dunas for me. Most of the 18 holes cascade down and back up dunes again, with undulating greens set up on peaks or nestled into natural bowls. It is a course that uses its landscape intelligently and with proper respect. Trying to overpower nature here would be pointless and crude. Much like the villages that surround the area and Vermelho itself, the course plugs into the terrain around it to make it feel as if it's existed as long as the sand itself. As an amateur, whether or not you've had a good round of golf shouldn't be dictated by your score, although too often it is. For those of us who don't need to play golf to earn a living, it is the places we play and the joy those courses bring us that matter most at the end of the day .

As I sat on the terrace of the stylish clubhouse with my post-round refreshment, I basked in the joy that Dunas had given me. So it was with a heavy heart that I left Alentejo, a region of Portugal that feels artistic and rural, rugged but refined. Dunas and Vermelho encapsulate the ethos of this area and complement each other perfectly, too. Fun, addictive, beautiful and dramatic, it is a golf course that will stay with you long after you've forgotten your score.
