Bob Mackie on dressing Cher, Hollywood glamour and his tell-all book

As the fashion world reacts to the death of Bob Mackie, revisit his glittering conversation with Sabine Getty

By Sabine Getty · September 14, 2026

This interview was first published in 2022 and remains unedited.

Responsible for some of the most jaw-dropping red carpet moments in history, Bob Mackie has captivated the worlds of fashion, stage and film for nearly six decades. It was Mackie who created a newly-famous Cher's impossibly glamorous wardrobe, dressing her in barely-there gowns bedecked in decadent beading; he too was responsible for Diana Ross's gem encrusted see-through bodysuits as she claimed her crown as the ultimate diva; and he was the designer who Tina Turner looked to for a flame-inspired sequin dress as she asked the world, ‘What’s love got to do with it?'.

Almost 60 years on from his early days working as a costumer at Paramount Studios, Mackie has released a fascinating new book detailing his stratospheric rise to fame as the go-to designer for unbridled fabulousness. With a forward by Carol Burnett and an afterword by Cher, readers can join Mackie on his captivating journey in The Art of Bob Mackie, accessing never-before-seen imagery and endless behind-the-scenes gossip. Edit's Editor-at-Large, Sabine Getty, sat down with the man behind the gowns to talk all things fashion.

Sabine Getty: I'll start by saying who you are to me. I have two fashion idols; Yves Saint Laurent and you. I find that you created something in fashion that just simply didn't exist before and will last long after you go. It's completely crazy. Now that I've met you, and I've seen that you're such a humble person, are you sometimes surprised by the enormous and long lasting impact your fashion has had on women in Hollywood and fashion in general?

Bob Mackie: I don't know. I've been doing this as a professional for 60 years now and it's kind of crazy. Once the Cher show went off the air, everything calmed down for a while. But 10 or 15 years later, all of a sudden, everybody was looking on the internet, and copying things that I had done before. So it's always a weird thing when that happens, you think, ‘Oh, everybody's forgotten about that,’ and evidently they hadn't, or it’s that new people discovered it, whatever it is that I did.

Sabine Getty: Yes, there is a completely new generation of people that have your dresses on their mood boards, and references and all that. I can tell you personally, for my wedding dress, I had a picture of you arriving at the Met with Cher. I went to one of the biggest couture houses in Paris and, I'm going to be honest, it was very, very difficult for them to grasp the concept of the sort of show girl dress. How are you able to make a dress that fits so close to the skin, but with the weight of the beads, feel so light and transparent

Bob Mackie: Well, it was actually made by a lady that I had hired. We had opened up a whole shop in 1969 because we were not happy with the quality of the clothes being made at the costume houses in Los Angeles. I said, ‘The only way we're ever going to get what we want is to open our own and hire the best people possible’. And we had that for over 20 years, way over 20 years. It was quite amazing, because we had these amazing people, but they got better and better. I had the lady who had worked at Columbia Pictures running their work room, and she was working for an ice show at the time and hated the whole idea of the ice show. so I said ‘Well come work with us!’ And she did and unfortunately she was sick and died very early. It was too bad because she taught me so many things. And my partner Ray, she taught him things. But then we taught her things too. So it was a wonderful relationship.

Sabine Getty: If we had to look at two dresses, let's say the Marilyn Monroe naked dress and the Cher dress, are they completely different techniques?

Bob Mackie: Not completely. They were both made out of what they call ‘souffle’. It's against the law to have used that fabric here in the States because it’s so flammable and it's very fragile. So those dresses didn't last forever. They were the same thing that Marlene Dietrich's performance dresses were made of for her nightclub act. Marlene would come to town, once, maybe twice a year, and it would take six weeks to make one dress because she would get it all beaded and then want everything moved over an eighth of an inch. It was crazy, but I got to know her. I got to know what she was thinking and how she thought. She was very visual and very particular about everything that she wore. But that was part of her gimmick. Yes, she was a fabulous actress, but she was very entertaining as a performer.

Sabine Getty: I read that when you were growing up, you loved Technicolour movies and that you wanted to be be a part of that world. How did you know that it was through making dresses that you would live in that world?

Bob Mackie: I didn't even think about it. When you're that age, you just figure they’d come to work that way, that's the way they showed up. I just thought they were beautiful. And I'd always look at what they wore. There was no television when I was a little kid, you know, that hadn't really gone into everybody's house yet. And then once that started, I was watching things on television every day of my life. And then I thought, ‘Well, I could do that. That would work. I can do that!’ And I used to make sets and little paper dolls, the kind of things that I would design clothes for. I would put on the music like I was designing for those shows when I was eight or nine, I was thinking about designing my own show. And I'd have my little record player and I’d put the record on, turn the lights off and put a flashlight on like a spotlight. That’s all I ever did. I worked. I lived in a neighbourhood with no children. There was nobody to play with. So I never got used to playing with anybody else. I just did my own little thing to amuse myself. And everybody thought I was the strangest child ever.

Sabine Getty: But it was all your imagination!

Bob Mackie: It wasn’t really until I got to High School that all of a sudden, we were putting on shows and we were doing creative things visually. Then the teacher realised that I had figured out a lot of stuff early on. I got a scholarship to a school that had that kind of curriculum, where you could be a costume designer. And I loved that.

Sabine Getty: And it went sort of rolling on from then?

Bob Mackie: Well, it did because the minute I quit, I was given a scholarship. But then I said, you know, I've been here long enough. I quit that last semester, I just said ‘Oh no, I have to go to work. I'm getting too old to be a student forever.’ I got a job that first week at Paramount Studios. So that said to me, ‘Well, I guess I'm giving them something that they don't have already.’

Sabine Getty: Yeah, completely. It's so amazing how fast it happened.

Bob Mackie: You learn so fast when you're working in the business. You know, you watch everything and you listen. And before you know it, people are calling you.

Sabine Getty: Do you have any early memories of things maybe not going so right?

Bob Mackie: There are all kinds of those things, you know, there's always that dress strap that breaks or it doesn't fit quite right and they just finished it five minutes ago. You just do the things you have to do in that moment. You can take bits and pin it, pin it, pin it and say, ‘Now don't turn around darling because it just doesn't fit.’ That doesn't happen very often. But in the beginning you learn it takes very little time to learn all those things that can happen. But that’s early on in one's career and then after a while, you know better and you know what to watch for; to take it in or to let it out or to lift it a little. A lot of people make clothes but they don’t know how to fit.

Sabine Getty: Yes, completely. And do you find it's very different to design for someone who goes on stage than someone who's on TV?

Bob Getty: It isn't really, if the dress fits perfectly. That's all you have to worry about. You don't just say, ‘Well, it's TV. We don't have to make it fit’. No, no, we watch TV. Like, someone I used to work with. once said, ‘Television is like an X-ray.’

Sabine Getty: Yes, that’s so true.

Bob Mackie: In those days, the TVs weren't as sophisticated as they are now. You know, to begin with they were black and white. My first shows were all black and white. And then all of a sudden, all of the networks were going into colour. And of course, that was even better. But then certain colours didn’t show on colour TV. In the old days, if you had anything that had blue in it, like a turquoise or, or a periwinkle or anything, they'd all turn the same colour blue.

Sabine Getty: There was no nuance.

Bob Mackie: No, it was so frustrating to pick the colour so carefully. All of a sudden, everything was royal blue.

Sabine Getty: It's fascinating.

Bob Mackie: And of course, that has changed a lot.

Sabine Getty: I wanted to ask you about Cher, because you're like what Givenchy was to Audrey Hepburn. And somehow at that moment in time for you and for her, you came together and created a whole different kind of woman.

Bob Mackie: You know, when they finally saw her perform in a nightclub with her husband, and it was funny, and they were funny together and they were charming, she had so much going for her and visually no stars looked like her at that time. All the girls had big beehives or flips or whatever. And nothing against your blonde hair, darling, but everybody was blonde!

Sabine Getty: Yes, that was the look of the time. Everybody was blonde and had a small nose.

Bob Mackie: There were no brunettes. And there was nobody that had a kind of a Middle Eastern or Mediterranean look. The Greek girls, the Italian girls, the Middle Eastern girls, were not considered pretty. Hello! I mean, they're beautiful. Well, of course, at that point every girl that had long black hair and a large nose was then told they looked like Cher. You know, it's not necessarily true, people don't just look like one person but they were so thrilled that somebody was out there that looked like them. It just was a funny, funny time. And everything did change. I remember in the States, when she was on her television show for a while, I saw every young girl in the country had her hair parted in the middle and straight down. I mean, nobody had anything else. There were no hairdos as we used to know them, you know, where they're all done up. It's interesting when everybody decides to copy like it's the hot thing right now.

Sabine Getty: I find it crazy. I swear I look at those images today of her arriving at the show with the long black hair and it's still so fresh, so modern.

Bob Mackie: Yes, I look around now and see a lot of young women with straight long hair. There were some times in between when the hair got cut shorter and Sassoon did all his 1960s hairdos and they were all as symmetrical and all those things are fun. That's what fashion is about. But what's really annoying to me now is that I keep seeing things repeating themselves after not very many years, two or three, four years. All of a sudden, they bring out the same dress and they're all fainting over how fabulous it is. We've seen that before over and over.

Sabine Getty: I find that a lot of people imitate your dresses, they try to do the same style of the heavy sequins and see-through and all that, but you have something very particular which is this effortless balance between sexy and elegant. And I don't know how one gains that skill - do you have an inner radar?

Bob Mackie: I get a little embarrassed if I see somebody in a dress at the Oscars and she’s showing too much and looks uncomfortable, even though she’s pretending to be fabulous, whoever that might be. I never saw so much of what we lovingly called ‘boobage’ that night when I was watching the Oscars. Why do these beautiful girls feel they have to show their entire chest?

Sabine Getty: I completely agree. I know exactly what you mean. Okay, more questions. Sequins: gold or silver if one had to go extinct?

Bob Mackie: Haha! It depends on how you feel. I mean, if all of a sudden your hair is one colour, gold might be the best. I would pick gold, or maybe a soft white, not not a blue white, but a really soft creamy, white.

Sabine Getty: Great. Well, this is the best advice I will ever ever get. When you've designed for stars, do you always meet with the star?

Bob Mackie: Always. Yes. There are people that I never got my hands on. Like with Audrey Hepburn and Givenchy. It was such a perfect marriage that you don't want to disturb it, you want to just leave it like it is. I mean, a lot of performers are good performers. They sing well, they're good actors, they're beautiful to look at, but they don't really understand. And all of a sudden, you'll find an actress who has herself completely figured out. What's good for her, what she's comfortable in. I think Audrey was one of those people that just absolutely knew. When she had to work for studios that had designers under contract, you can tell if you look back at her work, the ones she obviously got stuck with in the beginning. Before you know it, she was having little dates with Hubert just to see if he could help her out. And of course, it probably took five minutes for her to figure out that this was the place for her to be.

Sabine Getty: To bring it back to you and Cher, do you think that she also understood this herself?

Bob Mackie: She had a good understanding of what she looked good in and what was good for her. I mean, she had long, straight hair way before anyone knew who she was. And it was amazing what she looked like, you know, and how beautiful. She had a beautiful body that was so toned and gorgeous. Just naturally, she looked that great. She looked great in a bikini. She looked great in the evening gown. She could wear almost anything. That’s what was fascinating to people because she would look different every time. And yet she looked like herself. She could wear a whole blonde Dolly Parton wig and still you knew who it was. You never lost her for a minute.

Sabine Getty: No, she's so entirely herself and humble and grounded. I'm a big, big fan of hers. But funnily enough, you're very similar. You're a very, very grounded person. I'm so amazed, and I feel so lucky to have met you

Bob Mackie: Thank you. Well, if you're doing the amount of work we used to do on a weekly basis, You better be grounded!
