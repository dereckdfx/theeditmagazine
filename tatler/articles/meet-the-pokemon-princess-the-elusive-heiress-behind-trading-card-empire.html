Meet the Pokémon Princess: the elusive heiress behind a £90 million trading card empire

Swiss beauty Jolina Gisèle raised eyebrows with a 007-inspired video showcasing her multi-million Pokémon card collection. Half the internet is convinced she isn't even real. Isolde Walters meets the elusive heiress

By Isolde Walters · September 16, 2026

When Jolina Gisèle steps out of her Audi into the searing temperatures of a European heatwave, she looks every inch the Swiss heiress, in a knitted sundress and Christian Dior heels, with a Cartier watch gleaming on her wrist. Her peaches-and-cream complexion is flawless and her long hair a covetable shade of honey blonde.

But Jolina Gisèle – if that is her real name, more than one reporter questions – is no run-of-the-mill heiress. Her inheritance is not tied up in Virgin Islands bank accounts or discreetly managed portfolios, but in a Pokémon card collection claimed to be worth at least £90 million.

Even Jolina’s existence has been hotly contested. In certain corners of the internet, there is feverish speculation that she is not human at all, but instead an exquisitely pretty AI construct created to hawk the collection. Not an unreasonable speculation because, until very recently, the world knew nothing about this 20-year-old or her collection. But everything changed in March, when she announced her treasure trove of more than 60,000 Pokémon cards to the public with a teaser clip: a video that had all the hallmarks of a big-budget, if marginally naff, blockbuster movie. In it, she plays poker in a slinky, scarlet evening dress, before changing into all leather, cartwheeling over lasers with a hunk of a leading man and finally reaching a display case holding a selection of her precious cards. The video, masterminded by her mother, Jasmina, was shot in the historical Dolder Grand, a luxury hotel in the hills above Zurich, and involved a stunt double to perform those acrobatic cartwheels.

The romance novelist – and Princess Diana’s step-grandmother – was the third best-selling author of all time, behind Shakespeare and Agatha Christie. Edit’s Isolde Walters visits her astonishingly well preserved country estate

It was spectacular – and wildly open to controversy. At Edit HQ, the video was pored over with one question in mind: was the flawlessly beautiful young woman in it real or predictably pulchritudinous AI? Ultimately, one editor decreed she must be real. His reasoning? He could spot a slight imperfection in her canine teeth.

‘We really wanted to reveal the collection in a mysterious way,’ is how Jolina explains the announcement – mission accomplished. Not only did the James Bond-style video (yes, the 007 franchise was indeed one of Jasmina’s inspirations) leave Pokémon fans and collectors alike scrambling to learn more, but news outlets around the world breathlessly reported on this enigmatic heiress and her priceless collection. ‘We wanted it to be glamorous,’ Jolina adds, ‘because we think it deserves that.’

I have come to Zurich to meet this young woman who is either proof positive that Pokémon cards are the new Picassos – or is, in fact, a big Poké-con. My visit has been shrouded in a baffling, almost comic, level of secrecy: every email signed off simply ‘The Jolina Gisèle team’; and an initial request (denied) to not reveal that the interview took place at one of the family’s properties. By the time I meet Jolina at a train station before we drive together to the family’s weekend home overlooking Lake Zurich, I half expect to discover that the internet commenters are correct and she doesn’t exist at all.

But here she is, sitting in front of floor-to-ceiling windows offering a glorious view of that lake. She assures me that Jolina Gisèle is her real first and middle name; she tells me her surname but does not wish it to be published. Beside her sits her father, Urs, who made his fortune through AI security companies and also prefers not to have his full name in print. While other multimillionaires bought yachts or cars or diamonds, Urs bought Pokémon cards. ‘Every Swiss franc we didn’t need went into Pokémon,’ he says.

Completing the trio is Jolina’s lookalike mother, Jasmina, the creative force behind the cinematic launch video. Dressed in a forget-me-not-blue sundress, she speaks little English but periodically interjects in a flurry of Swiss German, leaving Urs and Jolina to translate. It quickly becomes clear that, despite the headlines centring on glamorous Jolina, this is a family enterprise. And, as eager as I am to discover it’s all a fraud, the family unit strikes me as utterly legitimate.

Jolina is far from the enigmatic heiress I had expected. Instead, she seems shy and younger than her years, with a Pokémon charm on her mobile phone and a cuddly Pikachu toy on her bed. She first encountered the trading card game – associated with the massively successful Japanese media franchise centred around fictional creatures with unique powers – when she was seven, a timid schoolgirl trying to make friends in a village in rural Switzerland that sounds as though it had more cows than people. ‘I liked that kids from all different ages came together and they traded the cards,’ she says in careful, lightly accented English. ‘The cooler, older kids, and me in the first grade.’ She soon realised the social potency the cards could offer. ‘Friendships formed. The boys always had football cards, but for us girls, there was never a thing that created such unity.’

She told her father of her new interest. Urs, a trained engineer, was looking for a way to bond with his only child, who was, by her own account, very girly. ‘I was working a lot and then coming home and nobody wanted to talk about mathematics or physics,’ he says. ‘Pokémon was something which brought us together.’

Jolina and Urs would convene in the evening, look through the cards and he would then browse on eBay to find new ones. He started small, spending between £45 and £65 on the cards on theonline auction site. And then Jolina asked her father the fateful question: ‘Could we have all the cards?’

From servant’s daughter to ‘collector of kings’, there was no one like Margaret Greville, whose dazzling diamonds have adorned royal women for over 100 years. On the anniversary of her death, discover the preeminent name in regal jewellery, whose legacy lives on through the likes of Queen Camilla and the Princess of Wales

To a perfectionist engineer with a collector streak – Urs collected cars prior to Pokémon – this was less of a question than a challenge. Urs dedicated the next decade of his life to compiling a collection of these 2.5-inch by 3.5-inch cards for his beloved Jolina. By his estimate, he spent four hours a day researching them, searching for them online, bidding for them at auction.

What had begun as father-and-daughter browsing sessions on eBay evolved into something approaching an international operation. He cultivated contacts in Japan, relied on dealers in London to grade cards before they were shipped to Switzerland, and built relationships across Germany, the Netherlands and America.

He quickly realised that acquiring the cards was only half the story. The condition of the cards was everything. The Professional Sports Authenticator (PSA) is the primary authority on Pokémoncards and grades the cards on whether the graphics are properly centred, wear and tear to the edges and any nicks on the surface. PSA 10 is the ultimate grade. ‘Ten means no scratching, no bending, nothing. Absolutely perfect condition,’ Urs explains.

He will not say how much he spent building up the collection, but later says that the most he has ever paid for a single card is around £460,000. Small fry compared to the £12 million that, inFebruary, American influencer Logan Paul sold his PSA 10 Pikachu Illustrator card for, to venture capitalist AJ Scaramucci, son of former White House communications director Anthony Scaramucci. For context, the Jolina Gisèle Collection has two of these Pikachu Illustrator cards, though they are PSA 8 and 9, not 10. By a conservative estimate, based on recent sales at auction, these two cards alone could fetch around £1.5 million, and those are just two cards out of a collection of more than 60,000.

Jolina admits that, for a while, their views on Pokémon were sometimes at odds. Urs warned his daughter off actually playing with the cards. ‘For me, it was more the passion of Pokémon itself, and then for my dad it was the collecting. Then over time, of course, collecting became important for me as well. My dad taught me that I shouldn’t play with the cards too much to keep the condition good.’

Jolina was at times disquieted by her father’s single-minded obsession with building a collection of Pokémon cards. (Though when I call it ‘a collection’, I am corrected. ‘Legacy, not collection,’ Urs says patiently.) ‘At some point, I thought that my dad might have overdone it a bit. My mother and I were a bit scared that we spent so much money on the cards. So many packages came in each day. All of a sudden my dad was addicted to shopping for Pokémon cards.’

Urs insists it was never intended to be an investment; instead, it was nothing short of an obsessive pursuit, driven by fatherly love, to build a ‘complete’ collection of cards. It’s simply a happy accident that, in 2026, by his estimate it is worth at least £90 million. Independent analysts suggest it may be worth about £100 million.

The figures are truly dizzying, but not implausible in a market where a single Pokémon card has sold for more than £10 million. Urs admits he got lucky: Pokémon cards have soared in value. From 2004 to 2020, card prices rose by 282 per cent, according to an index compiled by Collectors, which owns PSA. In the past six years, prices have risen by an incredible 1,350 per cent in this highly speculative stock market of sorts. Many of the cards Urs purchased on eBay are now ‘worth 10 or sometimes 100 times more.’

According to the family’s account, it wasn’t until last October that they realised the magnitude and value of the cards crammed into the cupboards of their home. ‘Everywhere in the house was Pokémon; you could open doors and the cards were there,’ Urs says. The collection was, understandably, swiftly moved to a secure location.

And five months later, the family announced its existence to the world via that astonishing video. Jolina tells me that they decided to reveal the collection to celebrate Pokémon’s 30th anniversary. This doesn’t quite make sense: Pokémon fans cannot visit the collection and the video raised more questions than it answered. Surely they made the announcement in such showstopping style because they want to sell the collection – I mean, legacy? They are coy. ‘We are checking possibilities,’ says Urs. But there’s no doubt they are serious. JP Morgan has been brought in to help check those possibilities. The investment bank is helping the family with connections and strategy, Urs reveals.

They do not want to sell the collection off piecemeal. It's just too much work that we've put into it, says Jolina. ‘The really special thing is that we have every single card and that took so much time, effort and dedication. It would be such a shame to break it apart.’

When I visit the high-security storage facility on the outskirts of Zurich to see the cards the next day, it is Urs, not Jolina, who excitedly leads me through the warren of vaults to the one that houses the collection. Filing cabinets line the walls and from these he pulls out box after box of cards, each with a dizzying value: one Umbreon (a Pokémon creature) card is worth around £230,000, he says; another set of cards is supposedly worth £2 million.

The climate-controlled facility is usually used to stow museum-quality fine art. But, as Urs says, ‘these are art, too’. (I’m not sure. The plastic-encased cards are almost the size of an iPhone, with illustrations, some foiling and pretty colours – not quite as soul-stirring as a Van Gogh.)

Jolina is nowhere to be seen as Urs digs through the archive to show me rarity symbols and the like. I later find her posing for a camera crew, wearing a Balmain minidress, chosen because the jewelled neckline mirrors the colours of her Pokémon cards.

I think, given my appearance, people think I'm pretty shallow and they often mistake my shyness for arrogance,’ she says. ‘I think it's really shocking to people that I could ever be interested in something like Pokémon.’ Despite the charm on her mobile phone, Jolina appears to have moved on to other interests. She hardly sees the cards any more; she's not allowed to bring friends, who have nicknamed her the ‘Pokémon Queen’, to the secure facility: ‘It's just not as fun as it used to be because it's so out of reach now. It's not in our cupboards any more.’ These days, fashion is her first love. She adores Kate Moss – the lake house has a vast portrait of the supermodel in the entryway and another hanging in the hall. Moss, she says, is a ‘style inspiration’. Her favourite designer? Chanel. She hopes to start a clothing brand with Jasmina, now that she has finished her German, economics and biology A-levels.

Meanwhile, Urs calls the collection his ‘life's work’ and Jolina's inheritance; he is adamant that his daughter is the reason the collection exists. That's why she is the face of it. She certainly is a very appealing and glamorous spokeswoman of sorts for a collection of cards usually associated with nerds and crypto bros. (But it's taking its toll – I hear she pulled out of a later interview, and Jasmina tells me that among Jolina's difficulties is the often underestimated struggle of spending six hours in high heels: ‘It was honestly really tough.’ Jolina herself says that this is ‘not her comfort zone.’) Urs tells me there has apparently been interest in building a museum to house the collection in New York, Japan or Europe.

He suddenly seems tired of talking about the soaring value of the cards. ‘We are talking now always about money,’ he says, ‘but it was something I could do with my daughter and we spent so many hours and days together looking for the cards. This is the main thing.’ So sweet, so parental. With a collection now worth almost £100 million, how fortunate that this Swiss tycoon spent a decade striving to – as the catchphrase goes – 'catch 'em all.
