What happened when Edit spent a weekend at the ultimate Venice Film Festival destination

Bond, Bellinis, and the Bezos Wedding: What happens in Venice, stays in Venice at Hotel Cipriani, a Belmond Hotel – as one Edit writer discovered ahead of the Venice Film Festival

By Ben Jureidini · September 3, 2026

I confess, I was sunburnt on arrival at the ‘best hotel in the world’. Such is the sole hazard of Hotel Cipriani’s personal river boat service: bobbing your way to Giudecca Island under the ponte del paradiso, you forget that, while the hotel’s famed privacy might protect you from the camera flashes of hidden paparazzi or autograph-rabid fans, it cannot protect you from the equally fervent glare of the Venetian sun. Thus, disembarked, I turn the handle of my suite, place my room key on the 1735 Versailles collection table, waft tenatively into the pearlescent ensuite, shrug off my linen shirt and confront the back of my neck infinitely reflected in the hall of mirrors bathroom, now the glorious red of the St Erasmus tomatoes I would soon be enjoying by the pool.

It's a problem facing families across the country – and driving the action in The Gentlemen, which returns today for season two – how does one keep an aristocratic estate afloat?

This is not the type of crisis that faces the typical clientele at the Cipriani. The beau monde are simply able to enjoy the hotel as a quiet sanctuary in the heart of the floating city, as unperturbed by UV rays as they are by meandering tourists or bloodthirsty hacks (sniff). Not for King Albert II and Queen Paola of the Belgians, the indignity of burnt ears; Gianni Versace and Pierre Cardin must never have felt the scratch of linen against raw skin. Privacy, done platinum-style, is the name of the game here. It’s why it’s the go-to during the Bienale. It’s why Bezos and Sanchez booked the place out for their wedding weekend. It’s why every A-lister wants a suite at the Cipriani during the Venice Film Festival, scrambling for the chance to retreat from a circuit of self-promotion and 17-minute standing ovations into a Bellini pink paradise (looking at you, Amanda Seyfried). Meryl Streep, Ana de Armas, Sophia Loren, Burt Reynolds, and Frank Sinatra have all called it home, and in 2023, La Liste crowned it the best hotel in the world. Where other than Hotel Cipriani could you walk down to the lobby for breakfast only to bump into Lady Gaga having a chin wag with Donatella Versace, Selma Pinaut Hayek, and the Clooneys?

None of that set await me on my own journey down to the lobby, following in the footsteps of Sophia Loren, Elizabeth Taylor and Mikhail Gorbachev (probably not simultaneously). But there is one star whose name is whispered by the guests as they cross paths on the staircase: Peter Marino. The giant of American architecture, who redesigned Andy Warhol’s Factory on 860 Broadway, who did the Agnellis’ place on Park Avenue, who completed the Getty Building and boutiques for Dior, Chanel, and Louis Vuitton, is currently stalking the mezzanine of the lobby in his signature leather trousers. He has every right to: he just rebuilt the place.

Sir Keir Starmer was supported by his wife, Victoria Starmer, throughout his time in Westminster. But how did it all begin for Lady Starmer?

Just in time for the Film Festival, the jewel in Venice’s crown has somehow become more cinematic. Arriving at Hotel Cipriani was already the stuff of Hollywood legend: James Bond himself moored his yacht in the private marina, grabbing a drink on the terrace in front of the 79 rooms (sun-kissed, not burned). Now, under the vision of Marino himself, the lobby has been extended outwards and upwards, a modern take on the 1950s glamour that inspired Giuseppe Cipriani when he founded the place. Receptionists in cerulean wrap dresses glide across the palazzo-style floor, and paintings by Emilio Vedova adorn the walls, a nod to the 1948 biennial, where the modernist was discovered by Peggy Guggenheim. Waltzing in off your private water taxi, you are handed your room key from behind a desk by Vincenzo de Cotiis. You are also, if your skin is currently a perfect match for the Cipriani’s storied bellini-coloured walls, handed a bottle of Dior aftersun. Miraculum!

As anyone in Venice will tell you, the best parties are private. La Serenissima is not the place for club nights or guest lists. If you want to know where the Film Festival afterparties are, then you’re probably not invited. (As for the Biennale, I’m reliably informed it’s the Dutch pavilion that knows how to send it, once hosting their celebration on San Servolo, once home to an asylum for the clinically insane.)

While everyone still waits for Chiltern Firehouse to reopen its courtyard terrace, has a new scene-stealer arrived?

One such exclusive invitation, no doubt hand-picked from the Cipriani’s unparalleled address book, brings us to the Grand Canal, where my (exceptionally well-heeled) fellow guests are granted access to the 15th-century Palazzo Giustinian Lolin, once home to the former Duchess of Parma and now a pied-à-mer for Vincenzo de Cotiis (he of lobby desk fame). A hidden gem in de Cotiis’ fibreglass and red onyx, the palazzo is decked out with oil paintings and chandeliers from the 1400s, its windows set with Murano glass. It’s the perfect spot for a party, but the risk of tripping on Venice’s rolling floors and knocking some priceless artefact off a table might keep you pious when it comes to the red wine.

Fortunately, a more secure footing (and far more alcohol) awaits when we bob back to Giudecca for another of the Ciprani’s new additions. Understandably, most people associate the Cipriani with Harry’s Bar – they were, after all, founded by the same man – but while Harry’s has become a little Americanised, the Hotel has just opened Il Bacaro Dior, a spot on the canal, where you can grab a Venetian spritz inspired by the 18th century explorer, Jacques Casanova, a ‘New Look’ manhattan with cherry liqueur, or an espresso martini, if you’re feeling a little less avant garde. Bacari, or Venetian osteria, used to be stop-off spots for commuters before they crossed the water on their way back from work; inspired, we finish our Dior-branded cocktails and trek a whole 75 seconds for some hard labour by the side of the Cipriani’s famous swimming pool.

Almost certainly, the Cipriani pool is the most beautiful logistical error this side of Pisa. It was Rupert Guinness, 2nd Earl of Iveagh, who had it built, back when his three daughters – Viscountess Boyd of Merton, Lady Honor Svedar, and Lady Brigid Guinness – provided funding for Giuseppe Cipriani’s masterwork back in 1958. Lord Guinness wanted a 50-foot pool built just past the hotel lobby, but through some mix-up in the Venetian department of planning permission, the Cipriani ended up with a pool stretching 50 metres, raised off the ground to avoid the high tides (come the climate catastrophe’s rising sea levels, there are worse places to seek shelter than the Cipriani swimming pool). To this day, it remains the only pool in Central Venice, which has a delicious kind of irony to accompany your backstroke.

Sometimes, among the bustle of Film Festivals, Biennales, or simply the sheer volume of foot traffic winding its way through Venice’s slowly sinking streets, the best thing you can do is get the hell out of doge. So, after missing breakfast thanks to a bed fit for a film star, it was all hands on deck for a sail on the Edipo Re – a sailboat fit for a soprano. The soprano. Maria Callas once whiled away the hours aboard the 16-metre vessel, as did Pier Paolo Pasolini, one of the great thinkers of Italian intellectual history. No pressure. By all hands on deck, I mean we sat down for lunch while our distractingly handsome chef, Giovanni, rustled up a lunch of asparagus and sea fennel, pecorino and wild samphire, and bumps of honey from the Venice lagoon. The cheese was served, he told us, on repurposed driftwood from the bricole that stood watch in the water. As, in fact, were the Cipriani’s room keys, which made for an unfortunate reminder that my own repurposed driftwood room key was currently languishing on the Versailles side table several miles away. This revelation was met with a judgmental bark from Fugico, the boat’s 15-year-old dog, followed by enough wine from Giovanni that it didn’t really seem to matter all that much. Anyway, by then, we had arrived at Ristorante de Celeste, the seafood spot on Pellestrina that sees locals cycle and sail for hours to catch a bite of octopus in red pepper sauce. Waste not, want not.

Of Sandringham, King George V famously said, ‘Dear old Sandringham, the place I love better than anywhere else in the world’

Back at Hotel Cipriani, the scent of jasmine wafts through the gardens, leading the way to dinner at Oro, where seven courses of local ingredients have been curated by Vania Ghedini and Massimo Bottura. The Michelin-starred restaurant is named for the way the sunset drips gold through the windows as it falls beneath the horizon, a far more pleasant way to catch rays than getting seared on the sea. Sufficiently satiated, it’s time for one of those private parties – a gallery, a run through the bridges at midnight, and a noise complaint or two. The Venice Film Festival might be a celebration of cinema, but the best moments stay decidedly off-screen.
