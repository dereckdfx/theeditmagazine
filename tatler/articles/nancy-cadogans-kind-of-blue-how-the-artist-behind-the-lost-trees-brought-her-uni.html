Nancy Cadogan's kind of blue: how the artist behind The Lost Trees brought her unique vision to a British countryside in mourning

With two exhibitions in some of the nation's most beautiful museum spaces, Nancy Cadogan is still mastering the fine art of losing

By Ben Jureidini · September 24, 2026

Nancy Cadogan is in a very long blue period. The British painter, celebrated for her still lifes, landscapes and portraits, is perhaps best known for the brilliance with which she handles the colour blue. So much so that a young girl, a stranger, got in touch with Cadogan and told her: ‘I’m in the middle of thinking about blue, and the only person I can talk to is you.’

Her affinity for the shade, which has inspired everyone from Homer to Picasso and Joni Mitchell, began while studying at the City and Guilds of London Art School. ‘Everyone had their own palette, even at that stage, even that young, because they just see the world differently; everyone has a palette through which they see the world,’ she says. ‘I really like blue, it’s hollow, but it’s opaque; it is both the void and the whole at once.’

The perfect match, then, for Cadogan’s way of seeing the world in her latest exhibition, The Lost Trees, which is coming to Compton Verney in October and November. The project began in 2022, with Cadogan exploring the ‘grief and powerlessness’ she felt in the face of her local trees being felled to make space for HS2. Each piece tells the story of a ‘lost tree’ and the stories it carried, but Cadogan is clear that her work is as much about memory as it is about loss – whole and void at once.

‘Within the actual story, there is obviously a sadness, but within the image, I hope there’s positivity,’ she says. ‘I want the paintings to be positive and uplifting ultimately, because I'm interested in work that makes people feel good. That's my general thesis, overarching beyond The Lost Trees. They have paintings that are forces for good.’ The paintings, she says, ‘are more memorial service than funeral.’

The Lost Trees comes to Compton Verney after a show at the Garden Museum, and in this new iteration of the exhibition, Cadogan is turning to more site-specific work, inspired by the trees on the Capability Brown grounds of an 18th-century Warwickshire estate, once the home of the Barons Willoughby de Broke. Compton Verney House, now home to a museum, reached out to the public for their memories of the local landscape on social media, and Cadogan set the stories to canvas.

‘It underlines the fact that people’s relationship with landscape and nature is not about ownership,’ she explains of the approach: ‘So much of what we think about now is whether you own the thing you love. You just don’t have that with trees.’

Zooming in from her London studio, she is surrounded by a forest of canvases, ready to be varnished and hung up on the museum walls. One depicts a mystical Mulberry tree, gifted by King James I to William Shakespeare after the monarch’s botched attempt to generate a British silk trade. (He purchased 4,000 trees of black mulberry instead of white mulberry, Cadogan recounts, ‘so the worms did not tuck in.’) After the Bard’s death, his house was purchased by a Reverend Gaskell, who grew so tired of the crowds making the pilgrimage to Shakespeare’s tree that he had it lopped down, ‘like an early Sycamore Gap man’. The Reverend, Cadogan recounts, was swiftly hounded out of Stratford. Now, the Mulberry tree is immortalised in midnight blue, temporarily sitting on her studio floor.

Another depicts the cedar that once stood outside Ashby Manor House, home to the Gunpowder Plot. ‘All those big country houses have cedar trees,’ she explains, not because of any particular architectural or aesthetic interest, but because they were believed to make the planter closer to God. Cadogan mimes one such virtue-signalling landed gentleman: ‘Not only have I built this large stately home, which shows that I’m wealthy and successful, I am also going to plant a cedar tree, which shows that I’m godly and righteous.’

Perhaps it was a similarly grandiose person who planted the cedar that grew outside Cadogan’s childhood home. ‘It was very much the thing of our lives; it had these huge cones,’ she recalls. Like so many of the trees she would go on to paint, ‘it was kind of blue.’ Then, when she was 10, the cedar started to die, wasting away in the English rain until half of it fell down, ‘like an earthquake’. Years later, when Cadogan moved back into the house, an Old Rectory, her young children used what was left of the tree as a swing until she was told by a surgeon that it had to come down.

‘I’ve kept as much of the stump as I can, but the responsibility of that feeling sat very heavy,’ she says. She phoned her mother, ‘feeling terrible’, and told her the bad news. ‘She said: “I just feel very lucky that it didn’t happen on my watch. I’m sorry it’s happening for you,”’ Cadogan recalls. ‘In a way, it was the passing of a blessing, I think. We did what we had to do, but I felt very, very complicated about it.’

So complicated, in fact, that she had not fully processed her grief – if you will – until she started receiving stories about the trees lost to HS2, which eventually turned into The Lost Trees, volume one. ‘I am sure it triggered such a visceral reaction because of this nagging feeling about the cedar,’ she says. ‘I always feel they’re such lonely trees. They want to go back to Lebanon.’ As does she: After travelling around Transylvania and Italy this summer, Cadogan hopes to return to see the cedars, which have been inspiring a whole new chapter of her work, back in their natural habitat, rather than transposed onto the country house lawns of pharisaic 17th-century aristocrats.

Life for Caodgan is currently like ‘a box of quality streets’, which is to say busy and full of treasures. In addition to the second iteration of The Lost Trees, she is preparing for an upcoming exhibition at Beningbrough Hall in Yorkshire. At the Bend in the River is named both for Beningbrough’s external surroundings – a meander of the River Ouse – and an internal landscape: ‘A moment of decision: you’re at the bend in the river, which way will you go? What are you doing? The internal angst, or maybe just a decision point.’

In addition to landscapes, the paintings in this exhibition are inspired by some of Cadogan's other great inspiration: poetry. Auden’s bucolics are a linchpin, with their currents that flow between the natural world and the internal world. One standout piece is a painting – deep blue, of course – of a lady sitting under a crescent moon (there was once a bottle of wine in the piece, but it has since been painted over). She will hang in the grand entrance hall among riverscapes and skies. Elsewhere, guests are encouraged to sit in a chair while readings of Auden’s poetry wash over them and look up at a painting of a book, which Cadogan captured to such acclaim for her Stanza exhibition on Lake Como.

‘I want the viewer to be both uplifted by that large Yorkshire landscape and the Ouse and the timelessness, but also allow the paintings to be food for thought, roots through into that whole Romantic sensibility,’ she explains. ‘It's about the release from the self, that sort of freedom.’

Of course, the modern artist has less time for transcendence than the Romantics. There’s far too much admin involved in preparing for two major shows for that – press obligations, for one. But Cadogan tries, where possible, to ground herself in the natural world at least once a day, even if just to clear her mind from that worst kind of blue: light from her phone.

‘We're all so busy in our heads,’ she says. ‘You wake up, and you're all cluttered with all your to-do list, and probably the first thing you do is you look at your WhatsApps, even though we all know we shouldn't. Then do I look at the news? Do I not? Am I going to get depressed? Am I not?’ Solace comes in the form of a coffee ritual, standing outside in her garden and listening to the birdsong. Cadogan has a motto: ‘If I just listen to the birdsong, I know that the day will be okay.’ (When she’s at her London studio, where access to nature is a little less accessible, she takes a walk by the Thames. On a recent outing, she was delighted by a grey seal, popping up above the water.)

Nowadays, the queen of blue is getting into browns, or ‘umbers’, as she far more delicately puts it. Trees will do that to you. Not only has she been trying her hand (literally) at soil painting, but she has been experimenting with paints created from polluted rivers, transforming scummy oxides into art celebrating the very streams they are destroying – and cleaning them up in the process.

And from oxides to otters, one use of Cadogan’s newfound palette of umbers swam up to her on a tour of Compton Verney. When a tree fell down in the parkland, it exposed a romp of three baby otters, later christened ‘Snap’, ‘Crackle’ and ‘Pop’ and taken into care by the Wild Otter Trust. It was, Cadogan says, a reminder that a seemingly sad occasion can still be a source of joyful inspiration – the otters are secretly included in one of her paintings, another of the antitheses that make up The Lost Trees.

Indeed, the entire exhibition asks us to embody Cadogan’s blues, to hold grief and regeneration together. The artist herself is continuing to master the art of losing. When the Compton Verney curator suggested she paint a Luckum Oak that, unlike the rest of the lost trees, was not felled but in the process of dying, Cadogan protested: ‘But it isn’t dead yet.’ The curator replied: ‘Well, how about you change your meaning of lost?’
