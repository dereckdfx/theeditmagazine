What is a good life? One full of Virginia Woolf, ballet and bisexual relationships, says James Graham in The Standard of Living

Award-winning British playwright and screenwriter James Graham debuts his latest play following the radical economist John Maynard Keynes as he navigates the post-war economy and his personal relationships as part of the Bloomsbury Group

By Hannah Kitty Brown · October 1, 2026

What is a good life?

That is the question at the epicentre of James Graham’s new play, The Standard of Living, directed by Sir Nicholas Hytner and currently showing at the Theatre Royal Haymarket. To award-winning British playwright and screenwriter Graham, a good life is clearly one of bisexuals, ballet, Virginia Woolf – and naturally, access to art.

Set during a time of mass economic collapse in early 20th-century Britain, the production explores the life of John Maynard Keynes, the clean-cut economist and intellectual who was also a bisexual art enthusiast, lovingly embraced by the bohemian Bloomsbury Group despite his sometimes straight-laced sensibilities.

The play opens with an affectionate exchange between Keynes (Rory Kinnear) and the Scottish painter and designer Duncan Grant (Thomas Flynn), a lover and, later, a close friend. As Grant bounces around the stage flirtatiously smoking a cigarette, he tenderly mocks Keynes – who is lecturing the audience about the importance of economics with ‘daunting’ graphs. The duo’s endearing friction sets the precedent for a play full of charming contradictions between the severe and the playful.

We see Keynes grandly explain mathematical ploys to rectify Britain’s dire economic situation, followed by jokes with his Bloomsbury babes about his hilarious attempt to ‘f*** Albert Einstein.’ Keynes is a man full of conflicts, his character skilfully depicted by the charismatic Kinnear, who embodies a roguish yet passionate persona that is difficult not to love. Because financial skill and a life of the mind need not be mutually exclusive; John Maynard Keynes founded and shaped Arts Council England, establishing the model for public arts funding in the UK. He used his economic knowledge to convince the post-war government to provide monetary backing for culture despite Britain's severe post-war financial problems.

Another contradiction: Keynes’s shock heterosexual marriage to Russian ballet dancer Lydia Lopokova, played by Royal Ballet principal ballerina Natalia Osipova in her acting debut. As Lopokova, she breathes life into what is, at times, a rather serious stage. With stunning choreography inspired by Lydia’s real-life routines, delightful period costumes (credit to costume designer Jenny Beavan), and an exceptional bluntness, Natalia’s role is essential in bringing colour to the arithmetic. Likewise, an honourable mention must also be given to Naomi Frederick’s dream-like depiction of Virginia Woolf. Although a secondary role, I found Virginia to be as dazzling and interesting on stage as she is in my copy of To the Lighthouse.

The play concludes with John Keynes' real voice echoing through the Royal Haymarket – his address on ‘The Arts Council: Its Policy and Hopes’. Keynes eloquently expresses the importance of bringing high artistic standards to the wider public instead of being confined to elite circles. His mischievous line ‘death to Hollywood’ tickled the auditorium as we burst into an ineloquent but necessary applause.

What makes this play most compelling is the palpable fondness that writer James Graham has for his unlikely muse. Graham is a leading voice advocating for working-class representation in the creative industries, referring to himself as ‘part of the 8 per cent’ of working-class people that make up his industry. He has also made calls for the government to restore creative arts subjects in schools; he frequently spotlights the crisis in arts education by drawing attention to the loss of drama teachers and the barriers this creates for working-class youth, and serves as a patron for Paines Plough, the UK's national theatre of new plays, supporting their mission to tour new work directly to communities across the country. It is safe to say that Graham is an advocate for the importance of art – rings a bell, doesn’t it?
