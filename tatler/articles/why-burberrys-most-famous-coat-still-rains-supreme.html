Burberry arrives at the V&A: the Duchess of Argyll’s ski suit stars in a new display focusing on the historic British luxury house

Ahead of today’s London Fashion Week show, the V&A opens the doors to a new showcase focusing on Burberry’s most famed silhouette, the trench coat

By Chandler Tregaskes · September 21, 2026

The Burberry trench is a true British style staple. For over a century, its silhouette has proven to be an indelible image of elegance and fashion prestige. And now it’s getting its own showcase. As the Royal Warranted luxury house celebrates its 170th birthday - and the fashion crowd await this evening’s SS27 London Fashion Week show - the V&A today opens a new display honouring its most famous icons. ‘The Burberry trench is deeply ingrained in culture – from Hollywood films and the arts to the catwalk, it has permeated the fabric of society. Proudly made in Britain, it continues to represent British craftsmanship while being recognised and worn around the world,’ says Carly Eck, the house’s Vice President Brand Curator, Archive and Heritage.

It was in 1879 that founder Thomas Burberry invented gabardine. Dispirited with outerwear crafted from oil, rubber and wax, he sought to find an alternative material that was breathable, but still weather-proof. To this day, Thomas’s master creation is woven in the house’s own Yorkshire mill. Then came the coat. In 1912, Burberry patented the trench’s forefather: the Tielocken, a gabardine concoction sans buttons down the front, with a double-buckled belt. Two years later, with the outbreak of the First World War, the style became popular with military men who praised the ease with which they could wrap it around their shoulders at short notice, preferring its versatility and lightweight material to the former heavy woollen interpretations. This was when the style became known as the trench coat.

George V awarded Burberry its first Royal Warrant as a tailor in 1919. Queen Elizabeth II granted her own, in 1955, as a weather-proofer. Then, with its wartime past behind it and with palace approval, the Burberry trench – a button-fronted piece with gun flap and pockets – gained its fashion coronation during the 1960s.

The gents had long favoured the style – the coat carried by Fred Astaire in 1934’s The Gay Divorcee and worn by Queen Elizabeth’s couturier Hardy Amies; the designer’s own is part of the new V&A display – but it became a stylish status symbol for women, too.

Catherine Deneuve donned hers in The Umbrellas of Cherbourg in 1964, and ad campaigns full of British pomp (think coiffed beauties waving from stately garden labyrinths; picnic baskets lined up in front of wonky-façaded Cotswold villages) would go on to entice more modish dressers to embrace the charming eccentricities of English elegance. The trench remained a must-have well into the decades that followed.

But the trench coat in gabardine is just one part of Burberry’s origin story. By the end of the 1920s, the British weatherproofer had started innovating with other materials, offering pieces of different weights and fabrics, like solgardine, slimber and solax. One particular material - Burella - proved the ideal choice to craft winter sportswear. One such recipient of its pioneering prowess would be the Duchess of Argyll (then Margaret Wigham), who chose a bold red ski suit when heading to the slopes of St Moritz in 1929 with the Marquess of Donegall. This striking piece of society style history now stands proud as part of the showcase.

Some 70s years later, at the turn of the century, Burberry opened a new flagship store on Bond Street, ushering in a new era. Swiftly, it began blurring the lines between outfitter and fashion-artiste. Christopher Bailey joined the house in 2001, three years later becoming creative director. Riccardo Tisci jumped over from Givenchy to take the reins in 2018, and Daniel Lee – the house’s current creative director, who had previously overseen Bottega Veneta’s metamorphosis – took over in 2022. Three legendary designers, one house, and one silhouette that just never loses appeal, now made in louche lamés, contorted into ball gowns and capelets, and encrusted in glittering gems. There’s never been a better time to get into the trenches.
