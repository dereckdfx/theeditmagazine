Meet the modern-day Medicis: inside the glamorous world of London's young patrons

What does a hot, connected, bright young thing with deep pockets and a penchant for culture need now? In this city, you’re no one without a membership card to a young patrons’ scheme in your wallet

By Isolde Walters · August 18, 2026

After dark in London’s haute cultural institutions, a new social scene is emerging. Insiders, on any given evening, will find a coterie of bright young things swirling martinis beneath the startled gaze of Whistlejacket in the National Gallery, or grazing on canapés after hours among the marble statues in the Cast Courts at the V&A Museum. Forget benefactors in twinsets and pearls, these modern-day Medicis are young and well-connected, with deep pockets and a penchant for parties, couture and access.

Welcome to the world of the young patrons: a fresh generation of philanthropists, armed with Amexes and ambition. They are, of course, being welcomed famously. The V&A, Tate, National Gallery, Serpentine and Wallace Collection have all created young patrons’ schemes. In return for an annual fee of around £1,200 and with the hope that one or two of these budding Peggy Guggenheims may in time become big-deal donors (the type with their name on the wing) there are prodigious perks. Think behind-the-scenes tours, invitations to glitzy opening nights, summer parties, studio visits, mystery dinners, rooftop drinks and lavish trips to Paris, Turin and private Venetian palazzos. For the young, cultured rich, it is the ultimate status symbol.

The Marchioness of Bute has announced she is closing her eponymous fashion label, Serena Bute London. The clothes – loved by Poppy Delevingne, Rita Ora and so many more – never looked more glamorous than when they were modelled by Serena and her daughter, Lady Lola, at the family seat of Mount Stuart, the staggering four-storey Victorian Gothic fantasy on the Hebridean Isle of Bute

The schemes fly under the radar with very little advertisement, so although they are technically open to anyone who will pony up the yearly fee, in practise they operate like private members’ clubs, with patrons coming via word-of-mouth and personal recommendation. And, like any hot scene, it’s becoming tribal.

You’ll find the financiers and lawyers populating the National Gallery’s Young Ambassadors programme, where there’s an air of seriousness and perhaps – according to one art-world insider – fustiness. The V&A’s set is rather more feminine, sequinned and, many say, fun, with the lure of a sought-after invitation to the museum’s summer party a key draw. The Tate clique is very south London, with a taste for social causes and conceptual fashion; while the World Monuments Fund crowd is unabashedly international. Meanwhile you’ll find fashionistas flocking to the Serpentine’s Future Contemporaries young patrons programme.

But there’s no competition when it comes to which scheme reigns supreme on the London social scene. ‘The number one has to be the V&A’ says Žanete Bukarte, a Latvian lawyer and hobby sculptor, who is a young patron of four different institutions, including the museum in South Kensington. ‘The V&A curated a very vibrant crowd, helped by the location of the museum and word-of-mouth.’ Euthymia Procopé, the social butterfly hired to scatter a little society dust over the V&A’s young patrons’ programme, is in full agreement. ‘It is the jewel in the crown,’ she declares.

Established in 2016, and inspired by similar schemes at MoMA and the Frick in New York, the V&A Young Patron’s Circle began with the long-term goal of engaging young people in the hope that they would become absolute devotees of the museum and ideally, budding philanthropists. With a wide age range of 20 to 40, the scheme attracted international students as well as established lawyers and hedgefunders. And with jewellery designer Cora Sheibani – daughter of Bruno Bischofberger, the prominent Swiss art dealer known for his close associations with Jean-Michel Basquiat and Andy Warhol – as a founding co-chair, the programme wielded social power from the outset.

But Procopé, the scheme’s senior manager from 2021 to 2023, knew she could turn it up a notch, and she set about making the Young Patrons’ Circle a hotbed of socialites, redesigning its brochures and bringing in Flora Vesterberg as her co-chair.

‘I wanted to make it really sociable, and Flora was great,’ she says. ‘She would invite Amelia Windsor and Princess Beatrice to the Young Patrons’ annual party.’ Freddie and Sophie Windsor attended two years on the trot, rubbing shoulders with Edit’s social editor Davina Chelsea. Society’s favourite royal DJ, Cassius Taylor, took over the decks in front of the museum’s medieval and Renaissance sculptures in 2023, while this year the patrons decamped to the V&A’s new east London outpost, where Tristram Hunt held court with Lord Ogilvy.

The strategy worked, and during Procopé and Vesterberg’s tenure, Viscountess Newport, Petrina Hesketh, the Countess of Belfast and Pamela Weinstock-Perry all became Young Patrons. But to keep the It girls happy, the perks had to match the parties. ‘The USP was that you’d get this incredible access to the museum out of hours… first-thing-in-the-morning visits and curator-led tours,’ says Procopé. There were trips abroad to tighten those all-important social bonds – such as the 2022 trip to Florence, which took in the Donatello exhibition at Palazzo Strozzi and a tour of Gucci’s private design archives, before finishing up with a drinks party at the former home of John Pope-Hennessy, one of the previous directors of the museum.

Romance occasionally flourishes between young patrons. ‘People do use it as a dating opportunity,’ Procopé says. ‘I know of several examples where romance has absolutely struck. You’d notice on day trips – people talking a lot and holding hands.’

But for socialites in search of The One, the V&A’s unfavourable female-to-male ratio is hardly ideal. According to one insider, the only patron’s programme to join if on the hunt for love is the National Gallery’s Young Ambassadors, as it’s where the men, with reassuringly high-flying careers in law, finance and tech, are. Former Edit cover star Sabine Getty teamed up with Harriet Roseberry as co-chairs to launch the £1,200-a-year membership in 2021. A year later, financier Jacques-Louis de la Beraudiere and art tech CEO Sam Glatman were among the eligibles on board.

Bukarte, who is also a Young Ambassador (the scheme’s name is a nod to the gallery’s famous Holbein painting), describes the hallowed institution’s youthful coterie as a ‘very elegant, very poised crowd, full of people who are deeply knowledgeable about the art world already, or hold very important roles in the finance or legal worlds. Very adult, quite serious.’ That comes with its challenges. One Young Ambassador, who shall remain nameless, describes the group as ‘academic’, with room for improvement when it comes to parties. ‘It’s such a huge space to fill – you have to have so many people there for it to feel like a vibe… There was a party for the Sainsbury Wing, and there were hundreds and hundreds of people there; it didn’t really feel as friendly and intimate as the V&A parties.’

The Junior Board of the World Monuments Fund has no such difficulty with its parties – the last one was a lavish affair at Horace Walpole’s neo-Gothic Strawberry Hill mansion. Data scientist Ege Gürmeriçliler, who serves as a co-chair, was the star attraction. ‘He’s gorgeous and extraordinarily rich, and the girls were all over him like bees on honey,’ one attendee recalls. ‘He spends all day working on AI models in his flat in west London, but he pours his passion into the World Monuments Fund.’

Buried in the Surrey Hills, Beaverbrook once played host to an earlier generation of Hollywood greats. This week, it was the turn of tinseltown’s reigning king and queen: Tom Holland and Zendaya, who celebrated their marriage by booking out the entire estate. The contrast between a serious (and often lucrative) career and the opportunity to lose oneself in art in the evenings is key for young patrons. ‘The finance tribe love the contrast between crunching numbers and then coming to something arty as a change of scene,’ says Procopé. There is also the ageless flex of art patronage – just as the Medicis realised the cultural capital to be gained from supporting Michelangelo and da Vinci, today’s young patrons are using the prestige in a uniquely 21st-century manner. ‘People put it in their LinkedIn profiles and on their Instagram bios,’ Procopé says. ‘People like the cachet of being able to say, “I am a patron, I am a supporter of this art institution” – and it does carry social clout.’

Then there’s the access. For a relatively affordable annual membership fee, society’s doors are thrown open to the young patron. You may find yourself at a World Monuments Fund event, mixing with the youngest generation of the Niarchos family or travelling with the heiress to the Bacardi fortune. ‘It’s a brilliant way of meeting people,’ Bukarte enthuses. ‘You get exposed to people a regular person would not. Most of these people are not that regular: they are very well-connected, or come from prominent families.’

Unsurprisingly, the Tate's Young Patrons are more democratic – they get a say on how funds they raise are spent by the museum – and they are more interested in social causes. While the young art aficionados have flown on Tate trips before, now a sustainability drive means only destinations accessible by Eurostar are on the cards. Still, they managed to net Princess Eugenie as an ambassador and arranged parties in the Tank Rooms at Tate Modern, rooftop soirées, and back-to-school drinks at the Arts Club, Ladbroke Hall and Annabel’s.

Gulru Arvas, an architect based in west London but originally from Istanbul, who served as a co-chair, describes the tribe as a diverse mix of professions. ‘At a young patrons’ dinner, I would chat with an artist, then someone who works in finance, a mathematician, someone in film and another patron in oil and gas.’ The dress code? ‘edgy, a lot of Margiela, that kind of thing.’

Arvas – known as Gu – has just become a member of the Serpentine Galleries’ young patrons’ scheme, Future Contemporaries, lured by the VIP breakfast at Zaha Hadid-designed restaurant The Magazine –‘it’s a really fun way to kick of Frieze Week’ – and the gallery’s Mystery Nights, where young patrons are split up into small groups, and given a time and a place to meet and dine in the fabulous homes of select art collectors. The difference between the Tate crowd and the Serpentine crowd? ‘They dress up a little more.’ Think all-black and very fashion.

Many young patrons can’t resist supporting multiple institutions, which can make their social calendar even more hectic, particularly during summer party week or when Frieze is on.‘There is a tribe that belongs to all of these groups and circulates around, going to several events per evening, running from the Tate to the Serpentine,’ Bukarte says. ‘That forms a community element in its own right, because if you attend these gatherings for a sufficient period of time, you already know who the other people are who go to these institutions as well. Often you share a black cab and run around London with these people. You end up going to dinner together or going to 5 Hertford Street afterwards, as many of these events take place on a Thursday night.’

With rumours that the British Museum and National Portrait Gallery are soon to launch their own young patrons’ programmes, and with the Wallace Collection’s Young Benefactors scheme attracting new members, the hot young philanthropists striding through the capital’s cultural scene are set to multiply.

The only question left to ask is: what tribe of young patrons do you belong to? The art might be priceless, but the guest list is the real masterpiece.
