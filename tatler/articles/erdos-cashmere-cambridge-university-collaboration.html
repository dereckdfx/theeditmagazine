From Beijing to Inner Mongolia via Cambridge: Edit ’s style editor searches for the chicest cashmere in China as a new collaboration with the storied British university is unveiled

Think you know cashmere? Think again. China's favoured manufacturer of the softly stylish knitwear is pairing centuries-old heritage with cutting-edge innovation…

By Chandler Tregaskes · September 22, 2026

Cashmere. Most Edit-ites will know it intimately. Whether wrapping up in layers of scarves and roll-necks for a bucolic jaunt to the countryside, slipping into some laidback-luxe silhouettes for an evening of purposefully undone elegance, or cosying up at home in the colder climes of Old Blighty, this wonder-material is tried and tested. But how much do you know about where it comes from? And what really makes it the nation’s chicest knit?

As Edit’s style editor, I’ve visited many a mill in my tenure. Usually found in the glens and vales of Scotland (think the Chanel-owned Barrie knitwear mill in Howick; Dior’s penchant for Johnstons of Elgin as seen at the Cruise ‘25 show; or Prada’s celebration of Harris Tweed Hebrides back in 2017), the heritage houses often boast of their appreciation of Gaelic craftsmanship and Shetland sheep’s wool – and, of course, the many wonderful things that can be done with it. But for true, buttery-soft cashmere, you’re going to need to travel further afield, to the other side of the world in fact, where the Albas goats of Inner Mongolia provide enough source material to keep Great Britain – and beyond – in even greater knitwear.

As a new collaboration between Cambridge University and China’s favoured manufacturer-cum-retailer, Erdos, is unveiled, I took the opportunity to find out for myself.

‘Protecting and evolving the heritage of cashmere craftsmanship is fundamental to Erdos Group,’ says General Manager of Erdos Cashmere Group, Ms Dai Tana. Indeed, the production of cashmere in the Inner Mongolia region is nothing new – in fact, nomadic tribes had been harnessing its warming powers for centuries. But today’s trade of cosy-chic fashion burgeoned at the start of the 18th century, when raw cashmere began to be traded out of the area along travel routes, before becoming a more commercially viable product, internationally marketed and created on an industrial scale, around the mid 20th century.

In 1980, Erdos Group began its life as a manufacturer of the material. Now it boasts five retail brands (ERDOS, ERDOS1980, BLUE ERDOS, erdos KIDS, 1436) ranging from mid-level to luxury, counting more than 30,000 staff and, as of last year, rolling over 60 billion RMB per annum. In 2024, it ranked 51st in China’s 500 Most Valuable Brands List. ‘Over more than four decades, Erdos Group has grown alongside China’s cashmere industry and has continued to advance its development through technological innovation and more sustainable practices,’ confirms Tana.

Innovation is at the heart of its business. While design and marketing operations reside in the Xidan region of China's bustling Beijing capital, the Erdos Eco Ranch – a futuristic farm-meets-laboratory in Inner Mongolia – provides not only a sanctuary for its hoards of Albas goats (the specific breed behind the world’s purest cashmere), but also a state-of-the-art technology centre and research facility that doesn’t look entirely unlike a sleek and stylish Bond villain’s lair poking out from the grassy plains of Ordos, Northern China.

‘The Group has invested significantly in scientific research, including advanced breeding techniques, intelligent husbandry management, sustainable manufacturing and new textile technologies, Tana explains. ‘[We] operate China’s only National Cashmere Products Engineering Technology Research Centre recognised by the Ministry of Science and Technology, focusing on improving cashmere materials, processes and products.’

It’s all pretty scientific stuff. ‘The Technology Centre has played an important role in advancing cashmere innovation. [Our] achievements include establishing industry standards, developing new textile technologies, improving fibre quality research and creating innovations in functional and sustainable cashmere products,’ she continues. ‘The centre has developed a full-chain innovation system covering raw fibres, processes, textile technologies and finished products. [We’ve established] research partnerships with 12 leading universities, forming a broad industry-academia-research network across the cashmere value chain. These collaborations cover areas from cashmere goat breeding, grassland ecology and fibre research to spinning, knitting, textile technology, testing and product innovation.’

‘A key example,’ says Tana, ‘is the Cashmere Industry Academician Research Institute established with Wuhan Textile University – the first high-level research institute in the cashmere sector to be established with an academic team. We have also developed postgraduate training bases, professor workstations and joint research centres with partner universities. These platforms bring together university professors, researchers and students with Erdos Group’s own R&D teams, allowing academic research and industry expertise to inform one another.’ Quite right: young men and women in white coats and clipboards are as commonplace as the horned mammals running amok in the verdant grass outside, backdropped by the rocky hills of the region.

Back in Beijing, those academic links have found themselves a new synergy – this time, as a muse to Erdos’s latest collection. ‘For us, the starting point for any collaboration is not simply whether two names look good together, but whether there is a genuine affinity in values and whether that connection can create a spark for our designers,’ says Sheng Ming, Creative Director, Womenswear. ‘What interested us about Cambridge was not only its history or its recognisable visual identity, but a particular way of thinking: curiosity, critical thinking, openness and the courage to question established ideas.

‘There is also an interesting balance that we recognise in both Cambridge and Erdos: richness with restraint, continuity with renewal – finding new expression within what endures. For us, that became an important foundation for the collaboration,’ she continues.

So, varsity-esque polo knits with embroidered patches and a healthy dose of ‘Cambridge’ insignia splashed across hoodies and tracksuits? I think not… ‘Our starting point was not, “How do we create a collegiate collection?” but rather, “What does Cambridge mean today?”’ Ming explains. ‘Our design, merchandising and marketing teams travelled to Cambridge together because we wanted to move beyond the familiar image of the University. We walked through the colleges, experienced the environment first-hand and explored the references and materials the University shared with us.

‘What stayed with us most was not one particular building or visual symbol, but the intellectual atmosphere – the sense that for centuries this has been a place where people have continued to question, learn and develop new ideas. That led us back to curiosity, critical thinking, openness and independent thought.’

Instead, the Cambridge collection positions itself as part of the literary-chic Oxbridge elegance, harnessing the enduring, understated appeal of great tailoring with masterfully crafted separates, oozing with highbrow, refined luxury. Imagine something one might don for a trip to the 600-year-old library, then onto a sceney bar in the winding alleys of the historic city.

Indeed, this line is not for tourists. But one recognisable motif did make the cut: the Cambridge lion. Typically splashed across rugby tees, tech fleeces and – of course – rowing gear, the fiery mammal now pokes out from the ribs of fine-knit vests, or dangles from gilded safety-pin brooches on creamy ecru cardigans. Roar…

The collaboration comes at a time when East-meets-West fashion has never been so prevalent on the international runways. Mithridate, headquartered in Guangzhou and directed by British talent Daniel Fletcher, proved a key moment on the London Fashion Week schedule this season; a showcase of Chinese designers is set to be unveiled to the European market during the Milan schedule later this week; and – just announced this morning – Jonathan Anderson will take his Cruise ‘28 collection for Dior to Shanghai in May of next year.

As for Erdos: ‘We will continue to explore the right opportunities for the market,’ says Dai Tana. As always, at the heart of the cashmere house remains a commitment to their belief that ‘the future of cashmere lies in combining heritage with innovation. [...] At this stage, our priority is not simply physical expansion, but building a meaningful understanding of Erdos among international audiences. Cashmere itself has a global story.’
