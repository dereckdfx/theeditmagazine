Meet Ringo Starr's artist granddaughter, Tatia Starkey, as she steps into the frame with her first solo exhibition

She is the granddaughter of musical royalty and spent time on tour with her father, The Who's drummer Zak Starkey. Now Tatia Starkey is adding her own branch to the creative family tree…

By Juliet Conway · September 23, 2026

Tatia Starkey has spent much of her life trying not to play the drums. This is harder than it sounds when your grandfather is Ringo Starr and your father is Zak Starkey of The Who.

At 12, while a pupil at the Marist Convent in Sunninghill, her school house was asked to perform a song. They chose The Beatles’ ‘Help!’ – ‘funny that, isn't it?’ she says – and Tatia, who was learning the drums, was put behind the kit.

‘I remember looking at the teachers. They were grinning and holding their hands up to their face, and the nuns were coming closer,’ she says. ‘Oh, I just wanted the whole room to swallow me up.’ She never played drums publicly again.

But she didn't lose her creative streak. She switched to bass, which she still performs occasionally, and is now in the midst of her debut solo exhibition, Invert, at Kaizen Fine Art in Surrey. It features abstract canvases made by layering acrylic paint and using her body as a printing tool. ‘I use the body as the brush to create the different patterns and textures and images,’ she says: ‘It's like a methodical Jackson Pollock approach.’

Starkey's studio is in the kitchen of her 500-year-old thatched house, in Surrey, where paint is splashed across the floor, walls and doors. The work began after the collapse of a five-year relationship with a fellow musician, whom she prefers not to name. Together they wrote three records and started a woodturning business. ‘We were very much entwined in an artistic sense,’ she says. When it ended, ‘all the artistic things that we had grown together evaporated’.

She spent the next three years ‘doing different forms of therapy... trying to shed all the skins and feel better.’ Then she remembered an image she had seen 20 years earlier: a couple lying entwined on a canvas. ‘I grabbed a load of paint and paper and my body followed,’ she says. ‘My body naturally gravitated towards expressing itself. I was healing without forcing myself into healing.’ Before painting, Tatia slips into a kimono – ‘They’re easy to whip on and off!’ – then uses ‘the fleshiest and most angular parts’ of her body to work across the canvas.

Invert originally referred to digitally reversed colour, like a photographic negative, before taking on a more personal meaning: she feels the world has turned ‘upside down and inside out’, from shifting gender roles to the pressures placed on women’s bodies and self-expression: ‘The art became a release.’

Ringo, who makes digital art and spin paintings – created by dropping paint onto a rotating canvas – owns several of Tatia’s works. ‘He took me aside a year ago and he said, “I truly, really love these pieces. I love your art.”’ Thankfully, it has resolved a present-buying conundrum: ‘What else do you get one of The Beatles for Christmas?' But he does not investigate the process too closely. ‘He never asks how they're made; if I use my body – he's still my granddad!’ Nor does he dispense advice. ‘He just cultivates something quietly... It's just knowing that it's OK and to keep doing it, really.’

Growing up, her mother, Sarah – ‘one of my best friends’ – tried to keep her childhood in Berkshire deliberately ordinary: local friends, animals, after-school clubs. ‘I didn’t grow up in the thick of the world of celebrity.’ says Starkey. Aged six, she came home from school asking, ‘So who is this Ringo bloke?’ Thanks to his narration of Thomas the Tank Engine, Thomas toys were plentiful. ‘As lovely as they were, I'm sure I just wanted a Barbie.’

At 14, normality acquired a different definition when Tatia went on tour with Zak and The Who. She remembers running backstage with the other children and eating ‘thousands of M&M’s’ from the riders. Eventually she became so used to the shows that she would ask to stay in the hotel, order chips and watch a film instead. Pete Townshend once threw a stool that passed about a metre from her face. ‘Not at me,’ she stresses. She thinks he was struggling to hear. ‘I remember thinking, “whoa... these guys are crazy!” Don't mess with The Who.’

Now Zak and Tatia share their work with each other. ‘We inspire each other a little bit,’ Tatia says. When she sends him a painting, the response is either encouragement or: ‘That is insane!’

She might have been exposed to musical greats, but Tatia insists she's still had to forge her own creative path. ‘Being the granddaughter of Ringo, I expect people will have opinions about how easy it apparently is for me to be an artist,’ she says. ‘But it doesn’t matter who you are related to. If your music is rubbish or your art doesn’t speak to people, you aren't getting very far.’ A famous family may provide ‘a small doorway in, but it’s whether you can walk through the door or not’. Her own music career began after somebody found her band on YouTube – she played bass with Belakiss and My Vitriol; she now performs occasionally with the latter and ONEFIFTEEN, a new outfit led by former Fat White Family bassist Joseph Pancucci. ‘Nobody’s ever helped me with my music career,’ she adds.

The family rhythm looks unlikely to stop with Tatia. Her 10-year-old son, Stone, first tried a drum kit aged two after Ringo gave him a small practice set. ‘He did a fill – a little run around the kit. It's really bizarre. He just is a natural, like we all seem to be in this family,’ says Tatia. Watching him, she adds, feels less like pride than recognition: ‘There's a link that keeps carrying on.’

Soon that family history will be dramatised on screen. Sam Mendes is making four Beatles films, with Barry Keoghan playing Ringo. A friend of a friend working on costumes recently asked Tatia what fragrance her grandfather wore in the Sixties. Ringo was no help. ‘He said he was just happy that he had a bed to sleep in at that time of his own. He wasn't thinking about his perfume.’

For now, Tatia is more interested in the future. The breakup that dismantled one creative life gave her another. ‘I really feel like I know who I am and I’m standing on my own two feet,’ she says. ‘I’m in a really, really great place now. I think that's thanks to making art.’

She is nervous about the exhibition – ‘I struggle with things being about me’ – but she remains optimistic. ‘I just hope people get a dopamine hit from the work, or they see something in the paintings that reveals something about themselves, or they feel joy.’

It makes her feel good, she says, ‘so I'm hoping that rubs off on everyone else in the room.’

Tatia Starkey’s debut solo exhibition, Invert, runs at Kaizen Fine Art, Surrey until 3 October. She is on Instagram as @vadapaint.
