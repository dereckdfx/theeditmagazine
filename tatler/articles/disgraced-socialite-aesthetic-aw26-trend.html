Style

The ‘Disgraced Socialite Aesthetic’ is this season’s most intoxicating trend – are you bold enough to try?

Wave goodbye to clean-girl neutrals and nights in, the season of the ‘Disgraced Socialite Aesthetic’ is here and it’s time to go out until sun-break and embrace haute hangover-chic…

By Chandler Tregaskes · August 26, 2026

She began with a stall at Badminton. Now Jade Holland Cooper has a flourishing fashion empire and £10 million Cotswolds HQ. Among her fans? The Princess of Wales, who stepped out in a Holland Cooper blouse this week

New York Fashion Week, February 2026. The Americana elegantes were filling the front rows at the likes of Marc Jacobs, Prabal Gurung and Calvin Klein to bear witness to the all-new AW26 collections. Michael Kors celebrated the 45th anniversary of his namesake label with a line of cut-to-perfection column gowns and elevated tailoring and Ralph Lauren conjured up a scene of earthy-hued equestrian elegance with nipped-waist riding jackets thrown atop flowing silken skirts and velveteen high-neck dresses. But it was a new addition to the schedule that set off the most zeitgeist-y pulse of the season: denim label 7 For All Mankind arrived with a fiery vengeance, proposing we bid adieu to the ‘clean-girl’ notions of bare-faced beauty and muted neutrals and instead embrace the charms – and qualms – of nightclub-hopping naughtiness and the rebelliously raunchy style idols of the early-to-mid-Aughts.

Instagram content

Quickly, it found itself a moniker - and a fanbase. As coined by fashion writer and internet anthropologist, Audrey Long (of the @xoxoposhipgirl account), the look became known as the feverishly catchy ‘Disgraced Socialite Aesthetic’. ‘[She’s] rich in scandal, poor in shame, and impeccably dressed,’ Long tells Edit. ‘The aesthetic is Sofia Coppola meets TMZ, dressed in a silk scarf and storming out of a family foundation meeting in Charlotte Olympia pumps. The clothes are glamorous, but they're never pristine. Think: vintage Chanel tweed worn to a mandatory court appearance, towering Louboutins on the courthouse steps, oversized Prada sunglasses functioning as emotional armour, and a Chanel Cambon handbag that still contains last night's gala invitation and a handful of NDAs.’

Ahead of today’s London Fashion Week show, the V&A opens the doors to a new showcase focusing on Burberry’s most famed silhouette, the trench coat

And it gained traction. Fast. Soon, TikTokers were making edits of the Olsen twins smoking on New York City paved streets, Kate Moss ducking into of cabs looking ethereally undone, Naomi Campbell’s infamous outing in Dolce & Gabbana donned to attend community service in 2007, and – most recently – faux heiress Anna Delvey arriving at fashion week parties with her ankle monitor in plain view. For Long, retro-Moss remains the ‘ultimate reference point’. ‘She normalised looking brilliantly disheveled in high fashion and proved that a woman could look even more compelling at 8am outside a nightclub than on a red carpet,’ she enthuses.

While much of the ‘Disgraced Socialite Aesthetic’ finds its roots firmly entrenched in the Beverley Hills scene (‘the early-2000s trifecta of Lindsay Lohan, Paris Hilton, and Nicole Richie is essential; they defined that entire era of celebrity’), Long credits the British interpretation of paramount importance. ‘The British answer is just as instructive. Sienna Miller leaving a Primrose Hill house party in last night's boho layers, or Tara Palmer-Tomkinson papped outside Annabel's at an hour she had no business being upright, taught the same lesson with a different accent. They never dressed as though they were trying to disappear or avoid the spectacle, and I think there's something wonderfully unapologetic about that.’

For Long, the huge reaction to the trend was ‘surreal’. ‘At the time, The Disgraced Socialite was really just my own observation that people were beginning to crave narrative again,' she says. The writer had first started piecing together what she was seeing both online and IRL nine months earlier on her Substack, but the NYFW moment seem to collide perfectly with her scene-stealing summation of the trend. ‘Watching it climb past a million views felt like witnessing the internet collectively arrive at a conversation I'd already been having in my head,’ she recalls.

‘As we headed into 2026, people were looking for a new style identity. After years of clean-girl minimalism, and the rise of homogenous identity (Parke sweatshirts serve as one example), there was a real appetite for clothes with personality and a sense of history that transcended wearing or looking the same as everyone else. I've always been far more interested in what happened before the photograph was taken than in the photograph itself. The Disgraced Socialite is really about that intersection of fashion and folklore. She's the woman who becomes more compelling after the scandal than before it. She treats public opinion as an accessory rather than a limitation, and she gets dressed accordingly. It's about reclaiming the glamour of imperfection.'

Now, those looks that call to the essence of – as Long puts it – ‘the main character of a multi-million-dollar tabloid scandal’ have hit the London boutiques. 7 For All Mankind’s satin strapless tube-dresses and bow-wrapped miniskirts can be yours for under £500 (but act quick, they’re nearly sold out); Valentino’s fur coats and hosiery are heating up Bond Street; and David Koma’s bold and barely-there partywear is already on the wish-lists of the capital’s most eminent It-girls.

For AW26, it’s officially good to be bad again. So, wear that hangover with pride. ‘Fashion has always swung like a pendulum,’ Long confirms. ‘We had years of restraint, and eventually people started craving glamour again, but glamour that looked lived in. That's really what the Disgraced Socialite is about. It's polished enough to turn heads and messy enough to tell a story. Sometimes the most compelling thing a woman can wear is a mystery...’
