Royals

Remembering Sir William Heseltine: the aide credited with revolutionising the royal family as Queen Elizabeth II's private secretary

The late monarch is said to have addressed Heseltine, the mastermind behind the Palace's modern approach to the press, as ‘my Bill’

Por Isaac Bickerstaff · 5 de octubre de 2026

Sir William Heseltine, the ‘trailblazing’ private secretary to Queen Elizabeth II, has died at the age of 96. Credited with revolutionising the royal family’s approach to the press, he is said to have remained close to the monarch after his retirement in 1990, after a 27-year stint at Buckingham Palace. Queen Elizabeth II is said to have referred to him as ‘my Bill’.

‘I think, like everybody who met the Queen, I came out of the room feeling rather uplifted,’ he said in an interview with The West Australian following her death: ‘One never felt anything but the better for conversation with her.’

Heseltine was an unlikely choice for the role of Her late Majesty’s private secretary. Unlike many of his predecessors, he was not an aristocrat by birth. The first native Australian to hold the position, he trained up through a number of roles at the Palace, including assistant press secretary, press secretary, assistant private secretary and deputy private secretary. His appointment in 1986 was seen by many as a deliberate attempt – spearheaded by Prince Philip – to modernise the Palace.

According to The Times, he was somewhat apprehensive about being appointed as the late Queen’s press officer back in 1960. ‘I’ve never had anything to do with the press,’ he recalled thinking. He was reassured: ‘They’ll probably consider that an advantage.’

When he came to the Firm, there was something of an iron curtain between the Palace and the press. His boss, Commander Sir Richard Colville, was described by journalists as the ‘abominable no-man’ for his forbidding attitude to questions. While this attempt to keep the press in ‘their place’ worked admirably for earlier generations of the royal family, by the 1960s, public admiration was slowly being replaced by increased interest – or suspicion, or in some cases, boredom – about how the Firm was run.

Step one? The (in)famous fly-on-the-wall documentary, Royal Family. Airing on the BBC in 1969, the year when Prince Charles came of age, the show gave unprecedented access to the day-to-day life of the House of Windsor. Lord Mountbatten had previously proposed a similar idea with his son-in-law, the film producer Lord John Brabourne.

Directed by Richard Cawston and narrated by Michael Flanders, the film chronicles a year in the life of Queen Elizabeth II, including picnics at Balmoral and lunching with President Nixon. In more personal moments, she buys an ice cream for Prince Edward and Prince Charles’ cello string breaks while practising.

‘It couldn’t have happened without Heseltine,’ Lord Brabourne later said: ‘He understood the point of the film completely.’ While other courtiers worried that Royal Family might have eliminated the mystique of the Firm, others were equally supportive. ‘Nothing could be more ludicrous than the suggestion that the Palace should have closed its gates to television,’ one is quoted as saying in The Times: ‘I don’t think the Queen found it difficult. The family needed someone to lead them towards making use of the new media, and Bill led the way.’

Royal Family has not aired since the 1970s. Heseltine explained why: ‘We put very heavy restrictions on it because we realised it was a huge shift in attitude.’

In addition to planning major aspects of the Silver Jubilee in 1977, Sir William Heseltine is also credited with inventing the modern-day ‘royal walkabout’. Before 1970, monarchs and other members of the royal family had not taken the time to meet with members of the public. At Heseltine’s behest, Queen Elizabeth II tried out the new approach on her tour of Australia that year, and it has become a staple of similar tours ever since. Ahead of King Charles own trip to Australia in 2024, Buckingham Palace replaced the term out of respect for Aboriginal traditions.

Born to a junior school headmaster and a teacher in Western Australia in 1930, William Frederick Payne Heseltine was an academic child with a marked antipathy to cricket. After graduating from the University of Western Australia with a first-class degree, he found work in the office of Prime Minister Sir Robert Menzies, serving as his private secretary from 1955 to 1959.

He arrived in Britain as a 30-year-old to work with the Palace. His Australian upbringing was much commented upon in the press, and he was warned that an application to Brooks’ members’ club may well be met with some opposition – though he eventually joined Boodle’s. Unperturbed, Hesseltine would go on to be made a Knight Grand Cross of the Royal Victorian Order and a Knight Grand Cross of the Order of the Bath. He was also a Companion of the Order of Australia and held the Queen’s Service Order.
