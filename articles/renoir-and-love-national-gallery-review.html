The pursuit of happiness: the serotonin boost of ‘Renoir & Love’, the National Gallery’s latest blockbuster exhibition

Catherine Ostler, author of New York Times bestselling The Renoir Girls , finds his new show in London a great breath of fresh air showcasing the mastery of the official ‘painter of happiness’

Por Catherine Ostler · 1 de octubre de 2026

Fifty or so paintings, six rooms, one big jolt of joy: the new Renoir & Love exhibition is a shimmering voyage into the wonders of late 19th century France through the painter of happiness. Here is the mystery and mastery of Impressionism; the social whirl of the Third Republic, its beautiful fashions, cherubic children, convivial lunches and flirtatious dancing. Plump babies clutch their toes, cradled by loving mothers, ‘Madonna col Bambino’ style; artists congregate and commune; lovers grasp fleeting moments, siblings pose in the country. The exhibition is a co-creation between the Musée d’Orsay, the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston and the National Gallery. In London it has already broken advance box-office records.

Renoir’s paintings were first refused by the official Salon. As the exhibition illustrates with ‘Study, Torso, Sunlight Effect’ (c 1875) described by one critic as ‘a mass of rotting flesh’, the French market was unaccustomed to his style, which broke away from the classical monotones of the French academy painters. There were haters then, and there are haters now. They don’t appreciate his palette, or cannot see how he could evoke the love of life, the relations of people, at his peak, in a way no one could match. But he changed the history of art, as Renoir lovers Picasso, Matisse and Hockney testified. As the author of a book about the subjects of two Renoir paintings I am of course in the lovers’ camp and if this celebratory show doesn’t convince the doubters, nothing will. As Matisse wrote, when he met an old lady who had been painted by Renoir in her infancy, he had a shock of recognising the painted child in the elderly face; Renoir had the gift of discovering ‘the essence of [a person’s] being’.

The show focuses on the first twenty years of Renoir’s career, from mid-1860s to the mid-1880s, before his popularity in America yielded huge financial rewards just as his health went into a decline. The exhibition’s curator Christopher Riopelle assesses his impact: ‘Renoir was one of the most subtle observers in any medium of social, sexual, familial interaction – a Balzac with a paintbrush – and his hugely original imagery of sociability is an invaluable record of rapidly evolving French society in the later 19th century. That it is also a key document of Impressionism, and thus of the birth of modern art, only adds to the wonder.’

What a wonder it is.The exhibition begins with his early bohemian years, when the hard-up artist painted friends, such as his fellow artist Frederic Bazille, with whom he shared a studio, and his then lover Lise Tréhot. While his palette is yet to develop the luminosity of his later pictures, the tenderness, the playfulness, the subtle body language is already there, while in his self-portrait you can see the stress that came with impecunious bohemian life in Paris.

He was influenced by his friends, but also earlier movements such as the Rococo; Watteau and Boucher also influenced his work. The brightness and prettiness of his colours seem to reflect his origins as a porcelain painter in Limoges; he always painted his canvases white and continued from there. There’s the charming ‘The Swing’ (the dress! Renoir, the son of a tailor and a seamstress, was a sometime fashion illustrator). And as a centrepiece, shown for the first time ever in London, the delicious ‘Dance at the Moulin de la Galette’ (pictured above), a harmony in blue and a magnificent swirl of outdoor dancing and dappled light that immerses us into the social life under the windmill of Montmartre.

He also captured the streets of Belle Epoque Paris, encountered in the newly built urban Haussmanian boulevards and the women of all sorts one might meet there. ‘Leaving the Conservatory’ (1876-7) displays his narrative artistry. A young man talks to a young woman as she clutches her music; how does he render the shyness and stiltedness of that young, hopeful greeting? See the supportive hand on the boy’s back; the blushing friend of the girl: even their feet seem to be in conversation, his one forward, too nervous to go any further, hers shyly poking out from beneath her dress. ‘The Umbrellas’ (1881-6) shows the transition in Renoir’s work; on the right, a little girl in his softer style, the left, a milliner’s assistant in his more defined mode.

But he might have been more at home beyond the city. The largest room foregrounds rural and riverside pursuits. Renoir’s Dance series was, as the curators describe, ‘the pinnacle of his treatment of modern love.’ His future wife, Aline Charigot, poses as the women in a series of three (two are here, ‘Dance in the Country’ and ‘Dance at Bougival’) of dynamic, innovative life-size pictures that show that Renoir was a maestro of mood and body language. And slightly apart here is his monumental ‘Great Bathers’, as surprising, experimental and controversial now as it was when he painted it.

To me, he is just as memorable for his most intimate pictures of family, and his interior pictures at close quarters. His tender portraits of children, with mothers, with siblings, include, here, his own, the Monet family, and the children of his dealer Paul Durand-Ruel (Christie’s Paris are auctioning some of his collection later this month), and his patron Paul Bérard. Renoir excelled at childhood – my book The Renoir Girls is based on two such pictures, of the Cahen d’Anvers sisters (Little Irene in Kunsthaus Zurich and Pink and Blue in MASP, Sao Paulo). He gives them, as he does here for the Durand-Ruels and the Bérards, character and spirit. He can even tell a story without people: ‘Bouquet in a theatre box’ is strangely affecting. Those beautiful roses – who are they for? Has an elegant woman rejected them, or are they awaiting a proud collection?

Renoir represents the beginning of modernity in art. In Renoir & Love we can all delight in his works outside of time. Enjoy those lovers, and those roses, in their eternal freshness, caught in a romantic moment.

Renoir & Love is at the National Gallery from 3rd October 2026 – 31st January 2027

Catherine Ostler will be in conversation with the exhibition’s curator, Christopher Riopelle, at the National Gallery on 16th October; nationalgallery.org.uk
