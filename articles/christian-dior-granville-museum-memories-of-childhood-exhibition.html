The house that built Dior: Inside the seaside home that inspired the couturier’s most dazzling creations

As a new exhibition fills the halls of Christian Dior's childhood home, Edit retraces the steps of a young, soon-to-be visionary designer to see the building blocks that paved the way for his era-defining style legacy

Por Chandler Tregaskes · 7 de agosto de 2026

Granville, France. A picturesque coastal town in Normandy where the soft sound of waves lapping against the jagged rocks harmonise angelically with melodic bird song and rustling leaves. Almost 300km from the buzzing pulse of the capital, here, you’d sooner find 12th-century chapels and cosy boulangeries than the heady stream of hot boutiques lined up along the Champs-Élysées. However, Granville’s fashion credentials are central to true style legacy. It is here that Monsieur Christian Dior would spend much of his childhood, slowly piecing together the inspirations and influences that he would later instil in his Maison and his lauded ‘New Look’.

It was around 1907 that Dior’s parents, Maurice and Madeleine, moved into a cliff-edge home in the region with their older son, Raymond, and a two-year-old Christian. Searching for a larger space to build their family, Maurice and Madeleine went on to welcome three more children: Jacqueline, born in 1908; Bernard, born in 1911; and Catherine, born in 1917. The residence – a Belle Époque construction surrounded by a hectare of bountiful gardens with views over the Channel and a coastal path to the stony beach – was the main residence of the family for around four years before they relocated to Paris in 1911. The Diors then continued to use the property as a summer house over the next two decades.

But its mark on a budding future couturier was indelible. Firstly, its soft salmon-hued walls juxtaposed with the dulcet grey tones of the gravel coastal path and the ocean sky on an overcast day informed what the designer would later describe as his ‘favourite colours in couture’. In fact, Villa Les Rhumbs – as the home was dubbed – might be the only place on the Normandy coast that one might wish for rain, just to see what Dior confessed he would spend hours staring at in his youth…

But the home’s facade and clifftop vista wasn’t the sole nebulous of Dior’s fascination, as its surrounding gardens – brimming with hawthorns, lilies and wisteria lovingly cared for by his mother – would deliver an infatuation, with the neat rows of rosebeds that centred around the dreamy dwelling. ‘I was happiest among plants and flower beds [...] my chief delight was to learn by heart the names and descriptions of flowers,’ the designer would share in his 1956 memoir. Later, he would cement their influence on his designs, ‘I designed clothes for flower-like women, with rounded shoulders, busts in full bloom, on willowy waists and wide skirts like corollas.’

Inside the abode, he would marvel at the Hokusai and Utammaro-style wall-painted depictions of flying cranes that he would describe as his own ‘Sistine Chapel’, as too he would pore over the mosaic tiles throughout the estate. In fact, back outside, one particular motif would pique his interest: a compass-style, star-like sphere, which has remained the central motif of the house’s fine jewellery collections to this day.

Years later in 1925, now aged 20, Christian Dior would remove the greenhouse to the left of the property and build his own pond and pergola. For a while he wished to be an architect, and some might say this early construction helped solidify his understanding of balance in design.

The family was forced to sell the home they loved so dearly in 1929, owing to the burgeoning crises of the incoming Great Depression. The town itself, Granville, bought the property to turn it into a public park. At one point, they even planned to bulldoze the entire building and prop up a water feature in its place. Thankfully, that idea never came to fruition and in the 1980s Jean-Luc Dufresne, Dior’s own first cousin once removed, convinced the local government to turn it into a museum dedicated to the legendary designer, as it still stands today.

Now, a new must-see exhibition at the historic address relishes the many tales of wonder that surround the property. ‘Christian Dior, Memories of Childhood’, puts Le Rhumbs in the centre of the story. Featuring nearly 250 items across gowns, clothing, accessories, parfum, objet and more that find themselves sprinkled throughout the winding rooms of the three-floor home, the showspace opens with photographs of the family in bucolic bliss in the gardens. Interestingly, the fashion on display focuses not solely on Dior’s own creations, but with pieces from successors, Yves Saint Laurent, Marc Bohan, Gianfranco Ferré, John Galliano, Raf Simons, Maria Grazia Chirui and the house’s current leader, Jonathan Anderson, proving the longevity of Les Rhumbs’ prominence throughout the Maison’s storied timeline.

But Monsieur Dior was not the first ‘Dior’ to reign here. His father, Maurice, would spend years running the family’s successful fertilizing company – first inaugurated by his great-grandfather in 1832 – during their time there, using the family name as its moniker. ‘Dior fertiliser is gold!’ early 20th century advertisements so read – now available to see in the exhibition.

Glamorous? Possibly not in the way the couturier Dior would have enjoyed, but the company itself did boast 500 employees by 1905, and – as the exhibition suggests – later gave the budding designer an idea as to how he could use the Dior name successfully, having seen those four letters pasted pointedly on hessian sacks and exhibition stands. The exhibition highlights this by placing this historically interesting nook alongside a number of ‘logomania’ pieces – including Dior’s own CD monogram, which later became Bohan’s ‘Miss Dior’ patterned silks and the 2000s-favourite Oblique prints – further illustrating his future-proof vision for building a world-class brand with global appeal.

Upstairs, an area is dedicated to the locale of the oceanic idyll itself, a former port town and popular holiday resort, and is now rightly portrayed as a protagonist of Dior’s own story arch. Along with the many floral interpretations in the designer’s early work, nautical riffs appeared as a recurring theme in his maiden collections. Sailor’s collars, triangular lapels and pea coats became part of the early Dior DNA. But the lifestyle of Granville played an equal starring role. Some of his first designs pre-becoming the Dior the fashion glitterati would soon know and cherish, were costumes for his friends for the local carnival. In fact, many of his local pals would become senior figures in his empire, as seen in childhood snaps of the designer standing beside Suzanne Luling (later his Director of Sales) and Serge Heftler-Louiche (his inaugural Director of Parfum).

Those whose museum-touring tendencies love nothing more than to gaze, gander and gush at gorgeous gowns will not be disappointed. There is - rather fabulously - a space in honour of the celebrations and society soirées that Dior would turn his hand to dressing the elegant, aristocratic attendees of. Jewel-toned brocade concoctions of lavish puffball waists and decorous embroidered ornamentations steal the show, as worn by the likes of Princess Lilian of Rethy at sceney balls that were also attended by contemporary tastemakers of Cecil Beaton, Jane Birkin and Baron Guy de Rothschild. A particular favourite event of his during this era was art collector Charles de Beistegui’s extravagant costume ‘ball of the century’ in 1951 hosted at Venice’s Palazzo Labia of which he claimed, ‘This was the most marvellous spectacle which I have ever seen, or ever shall see.’ A sparkling masquerade mask created by the designer can be seen as part of the show.

Funny to think that such all that glamour, grandeur and glitz - and the decades of shimmering chic that followed - could have looked very different if it wasn’t for a pink-walled house on a clifftop in sleepy Granville… Hop the Channel, head West and witness where it all began.

‘Christian Dior, Memories of Childhood’ is open until the 1st November 2026 at the Musée Christian Dior
