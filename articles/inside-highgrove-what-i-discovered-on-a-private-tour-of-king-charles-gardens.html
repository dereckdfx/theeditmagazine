A glimpse inside Highgrove: what I discovered on a private tour of King Charles' gardens

From the treehouse where Princes William and Harry played as children to the Queen Mother's favourite shade of pink, Highgrove offers a glimpse of the family life behind the royal residence

Por Isolde Walters · 9 de octubre de 2026

This weekend, the great and gilded of Britain's cultural scene will gather for the Cliveden Literary Festival. But the estate boasts a history with more twists and turns than even the most gripping page-turner, writes Natalie Livingstone

When the invitation arrived offering a private tour of the King’s gardens at Highgrove – known to be his most personal residence – I didn't hesitate.

This Cotswolds estate is where Prince William and Prince Harry spent much of their childhood and where the private reunion between the King, Prince Harry, the Duchess of Sussex, Prince Archie and Princess Lilibet took place this summer.

Many consider the 15 acres of interlinked gardens, which the monarch has worked away on for over 40 years, to be his most cherished creative project. In fact, the gardener Alan Titchmarsh has called these gardens one of the King’s ‘crowning glories’, lovingly nurtured from the very dilapidated state the estate was in when he purchased it in 1980.

It’s a quick drive from Kemble to the royal estate, and the first stop is the Orchard Room. This elegant traditional-style pavilion building often serves as a restaurant, though the personal touches ensure you never forget where you are. Paintings of Queen Camilla, the King himself, the late Queen, and Princes William and Harry hang on the butter-yellow-painted walls. In an adjoining room, family photographs sit on chests of drawers and side tables: the wedding of the Duke and Duchess of Sussex, the Prince and Princess of Wales with their three children. My favourite? The King, in Hunter boots, carrying two buckets, cheerily leading the way as a flock of chickens follow.

After canapés and sampling the new Highgrove-approved Bottle Green flavours – I’m here to celebrate the cordial new range inspired by the Highgrove gardens and in partnership with The King’s Foundation – I’m ushered out on my private tour.

We begin by circling the Georgian manor house, before catching a glimpse of the sundial garden. Sadly, the October drizzly day does not show this garden to full effect – best come in the summer to see the delphiniums in showstopping array. But, I appreciate the yew hedges clipped into ‘windows’ that reveal busts of the King captured at various stages of his life. Who wouldn’t want to populate their garden with statues of themselves at different points of life?

En route to the kitchen garden, I spot a stone dog, folded up and asleep, chiselled into a red-brick wall. Our very knowledgeable guide tells me that this hound was once Tigga, better known as ‘Tigga the digger’, the King’s adored Jack Russell, a rescue from Battersea, with a passion for pawing at dirt (which meant he was absolutely not allowed into the kitchen garden). Sadly deceased now, he’s still remembered at Highgrove, memorialised with this sculpture.

He’s the grandson of Grace Kelly, the eldest son of Prince Albert II of Monaco, and for 20 years he has kept his life private. But what happened when Alexandre Grimaldi went public?

Stepping inside the kitchen garden, I notice it’s a little warmer, thanks to the thick, enclosing walls that mean the walled garden is always a couple of degrees warmer than the rest of the estate. In the centre of the garden is a moss-encrusted fountain and surrounding it, in carefully laid plots, are formal beds of salad crops, asparagus, onions and potatoes, beans and peas as well as carrots and parsnips. The King’s enthusiastic adoption of organic farming is well known and, true to form, the gardens at Highgrove are entirely organic.

Fruit trees grow in lines and are trained around iron hoops that form attractive archways overhead. The neat gravel path runs straight to a gate painted a peachy pink shade. That colour was the Queen Mother’s favourite, I’m told. The King was famously devoted to his grandmother and her influence is felt around Highgrove, including in the next area we visit – the stumpery – where there’s an engraved portrait of the deceased royal, in her gardening outfit, which included a string of pearls, naturally.

The so-called stumpery is a nod to the Victorian tradition of growing ferns among upturned tree stumps. The result is a quiet, damp (but in a good way) garden with a strangely still atmosphere. I spot the Hobbit-esque tree-house the young princes played in as children; it was brought to Highgrove when William was six years old.

The last stop has the best view of all: the lily pool garden with the manor house lying in the distance beyond. Two rococo-style benches painted a blush pink and a true blue inject a playful splash of colour into the otherwise verdant landscape of autumnal leaves turning and neatly cropped topiary. Looking at the house, so perfectly situated in thoughtfully designed gardens, full of personal details and nods to the King’s life and his family, it isn’t difficult to see why the monarch has chosen to make Highgrove the most homely of his residences.

And with that, the only thing left to do is a quick raid on the gift shop in the hope of bringing a little royal flair back to my flat in South London. Whether I'll succeed remains to be seen, but Highgrove offers inspiration aplenty for the green-fingered and not-so-green-fingered alike.
