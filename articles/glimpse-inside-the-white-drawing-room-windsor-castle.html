Royals

A glimpse inside the White Drawing Room at Windsor Castle: the gilded heart of royal life and historic events – both celebratory and solemn

From Prince William's confirmation to Princess Eugenie's wedding and Queen Elizabeth II's first Christmas without Prince Philip, the White Drawing Room at Windsor Castle has been at the centre of generations of royal events

Por Ben Jureidini · 6 de octubre de 2026

Windsor Castle is steeped in history, a monument to nearly 1000 years of British royalty. Even under those storied ceilings, though, perhaps no room has witnessed more than the White Drawing Room.

From landmark speeches delivered by Queen Elizabeth II to a bevvy of royal weddings, this lavishly decorated location has been at the heart of the Firm’s finest hours and darkest times. Here, Edit uncovers the hidden secrets of Windsor Castle’s White Drawing Room.

When the world’s most elusive painter met the ultimate It-girl, it should have been cinematic gold…

Dating back to the reign of King George IV, who constructed three semi-state rooms as the site of his private apartments, the White Drawing Room features interiors from Morel & Seddon – indeed, the famous crafts partners came together following Morel’s commission by the King for the new rooms at Windsor. The pair were responsible for the ornate gold gilt doors, often seen when a monarch formally entertains a head of state or fellow royal, like when King Charles welcomed Ursula von der Leyen in 2023. Using George IV’s rooms to entertain visitors was a tradition started by Queen Elizabeth II, and one that William and Kate, like King Charles, have taken up themselves, as evidenced when they welcomed Rajwa and Hussein by the drawing room’s plush white sofas.

Adding a personal touch, George IV brought some decorative pieces from his London residence, Carlton House – including a Remond mantel clock, which sits atop the fireplace and is decorated with an allegorical depiction of Astronomy. Some of the furniture, including an oak cabinet veneered with mahogany, tulipwood, boxwood, and purplewood, was designed by Martin Carlin, the great 18th-century craftsman, and is adorned with brocatello marble and gilt bronze mounts. Etienne Falconet candelabras decorate the walls, as does a Franz Winterhalter portrait of Queen Victoria in her Garter regalia, painted in 1842.

With all of this grandeur, it's no wonder that members of the royal family have graced the White Drawing Room on some of the biggest days of their lives. Prince William's official confirmation portraits were taken in the room, alongside his royal godparents and family members. When Princess Eugenie tied the knot with Jack Brooksbank in 2018, the couple chose for Alex Bramall to take their official photos in the drawing room, surrounded by the golden gilt frames and the stunning fireplace, alongside Prince Beatrice, the Duke and Duchess of York, Prince George, Princess Charlotte, Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip. King Charles, too, chose the White Drawing Room for his wedding photos after he married Queen Camilla in 2005.

But not every moment in the room has been quite so joyous. Indeed, it was the site of the late Queen’s historic address to the nation during the Coronavirus lockdown of 2020, when she and Prince Philip were both in joint isolation at Windsor Castle. The varnished desk, traditionally laden with grinning family photographs, was nearly laid bare, decorated with only a silver pen pot, a vase of red roses, and a white ceramic table lamp that has been in the White Drawing Room for over 35 years – previously seen in a photograph marking Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip’s ruby wedding anniversary in 1987.

But the White Drawing Room has survived many a disaster. The Axminster Carpets rug, which dates back to at least 1890, was originally housed in the Green Drawing Room. It suffered smoke damage during the Great Fire of Windsor Castle of 1992, however, and had to undergo significant repairs before it could be fitted in the White Drawing Room, where it decorates the floors to this day.

It’s clear that the White Drawing Room will be the glamorous heart of British diplomacy for years – perhaps centuries – to come.
