Hay Fever review: Christine Baranski sparkles in this Noël Coward classic

The Gilded Age star and Richard E. Grant head up a talented ensemble in this flawlessly acted, if featherlight, revival of Hay Fever , Noël Coward's country house farce

Por Isolde Walters · 2 de octubre de 2026

If you like a classic Noël Coward comedy of manners, you are going to love Hay Fever at the Wyndham, with Christine Baranski as the former actress turned country house hostess who can’t retire from histrionics.

We join the Bliss family for a rather crowded weekend at their home on the River Thames. Judith Bliss (Baranski), who can’t quite let go of the stage, has invited her potential lover Sandy Tyrell (Ash Hunter). To her apparent dismay, her wonderfully stroppy son Simon (Will Close) has done likewise, inviting his cougar paramour Myra Arundel (Nancy Carroll) – as has her daughter Sorel (Ophelia Lovibond), who has asked the buttoned-up diplomat Richard Greatham (Charles Edwards) for a weekend of promised passion. Bliss’s novelist husband David (Richard E. Grant) has amorous designs on the young, wide-eyed flapper Jackie Coryton (Lucy Karczewski) who arrives to do who-knows-what with him. And so the stage is set for a comedy of theatrical flair, self-absorption, social faux pas, lover-swapping and farce, in this production directed by Emily Burns.

As Rivals returns, Cotswold fever hits a new high – and with Hollywood blockbusters behind her and Broadway ahead, Hayley Atwell is ruffling feathers in Rutshire as the former Mrs Rupert Campbell-Black

It’s wonderfully executed. Baranski is marvellous as the perennial actress, who can’t help but force plots, milk moments and strike poses. The set is hung with framed posters of her billboard productions, and she repeatedly threatens to return to the stage – of course, the joke is: she never left. Baranski plays the role to perfection: the expansive sweep of her arms as she floats around in a gold sequinned cape, the arch glances. In one very funny moment, at the press view, she imagined with one raised brow, ‘the satisfied gurgle of the Daily Mail’, before cocking a finger into the audience, to a widespread laugh.

Close is a stand-out. He plays Simon Bliss with Rik Mayall-esque slouching insouciance, loping across the stage, pulling faces and leaving us in no doubt that he truly is Judith's son. His physical comedy was the best I’ve seen; he made the audience roar with a mere twist of an arm and pout of a lip. Brilliant. But then the entire cast is flawless. Grant plays his part with Withnailian flourish, stalking across the stage and seducing. Lovibond as Sorel holds her own as the one Bliss family member with a modicum of self-knowledge: that they are all pretty beastly, full of artifice. Of course, as is often the case, self-knowledge avails her nothing. She can’t climb out of the dysfunction, but she’s not as ghastly as her mother – yet.

Catherine Ostler, author of New York Times bestselling The Renoir Girls, finds his new show in London a great breath of fresh air showcasing the mastery of the official ‘painter of happiness’

There’s a wonderful moment when Richard Greatham (Edwards) and Jackie (Karczewski) are left alone in the interwar sitting room set, trading interminable small talk about all the foreign climes Greatham has visited and Jackie hasn’t. Edwards is superb as the diplomat who finds the Bliss histrionics at first attractive and then alarming. Karczewski shines as the fish-out-of-water marooned among these ‘intellectuals’. She gets a great laugh when, as part of a parlour game, she’s implored to come up with an adjective – saucily, say, or winsomely. She gets very het up before finally yelping her contribution: ‘Appendicitis!’

Hunter’s Sandy Tyrell doesn’t get quite as much a chance to shine as the other characters do, jealously seizing their limelight wherever they spot an opportunity, but he rounds out a vastly talented ensemble, perfectly playing off each other to bring this much-loved play to life.

My verdict is that if you are a fan of Noël Coward, you will love this production of Hay Fever. The cast could not do it better. Baranski is the definitive Judith, wafting around her sitting room like it’s a West End stage (which, of course, it is), perfectly supported by actors performing at the very tippity top of their game. Did I leave the theatre feeling stirred by commentary on the human condition? No. But then, that’s not what you get from Coward, in my view. His plays are perfectly spun, clever constructions that leave me feeling like I’ve just consumed a bundle of candy floss: all fizzy sugar, no substance. Did I wish someone would break a window or commit some act of violence and let a little fresh air into this very traditional period piece? Well, yes. But it's flawlessly acted, the jokes land, and if you want to hear the hits, get your tickets now.

Hay Fever is at the Wyndham Theatre until December 12, hayfeverplay.com
