Beyond belief: inside Damu at the Mandrake, London’s most mystical spa

If your aura is flagging, or you’re craving a little occult glamour, the Mandrake promises an otherworldly sort of rejuvenation in the heart of W1

Por Tamzin Reynolds · 8 de diciembre de 2025

‘Sorry, I can’t do Mustique this month; Mercury’s in retrograde.’ You’d think cancelling a Caribbean trip required something more dramatic than planetary misalignment. But now, aside from nanny schedules and board meetings, it seems the jet set are consulting the cosmos. Some are rethinking their travels based on a horoscope warning and even changing or cancelling their plans if a spiritual adviser (yes, everyone has one now) warned it wasn’t the right time.

The baroque composer's dark opera is making a rare return to the Royal Opera House. Edit goes backstage to meet the mezzo-soprano - and Linda Evangelista doppelgänger- taking on the title role

Of course, London’s hotels have caught on, offering crystal healing sessions and resident astrologers alongside the usual spa menu. I’m normally sceptical of such mystical practices. My idea of a ritual is a morning coffee, not chakra realignment. That said, something about The Mandrake hotel has always piqued my curiosity. Opened in 2017, this phantasmagorical Fitzrovia hotspot – complete with ‘spiritual concierges’ – has made cosmic gong baths and ecstatic dance nights the height of cool. It was the spa, Damu, new this year, that persuaded my inner sceptic to give it a try. It claims to be rooted both ‘in the timeless wisdom of ancient healing traditions’ and in the transformative possibilities of modern science – a balance of mysticism and measurable data that even I could get behind. And its headline treatment, the Origin Pool, described as ‘the world’s first multi-sensory, zero-gravity wellness journey’, sounded intriguing enough at least to take a dip into the unknown.

Adjectives like ‘otherworldly’ are often the last refuge of the unimaginative travel writer. But stepping into The Mandrake on a busy Fitzrovia Friday night, the cliché actually rings true. Duck under the all-seeing eye above the entrance and a black corridor engulfs you, nightclub-style, in ambient sound. In the few seconds it takes to reach reception, Oxford Street, seething with crowds and commerce, feels very far away. In the jungly inner courtyard, giant ferns tilt over tables as frighteningly fashionable guests smoke and drink in the vaporous dusk. To one side is Waeska (think ‘ayahuasca’ with fewer syllables), a long bar where a surreal taxidermied creature – half-peacock, half-gazelle – hangs behind the bartenders, who shake up ‘ethnobotanical’ cocktails, combining rare botanicals and homemade tinctures. On the other is Yopo, serving padrón peppers, chimichurri steaks and mangosteen margaritas beneath the gaze of a hybrid ostrich-python figure that’s both mesmerising and bizarre. It’s disorientating by design – intended to keep the mind off-balance and the senses keen.

Supremely well acted, Arthur Miller’s just-over two-hour play at the Wyndham’s Theatre never lets up in its ruthless quest for justice. Here we have an All My Sons for the ages, directed by Ivo van Hove

My weekend retreat begins with a spiritual welcome ceremony on the first-floor terrace. In a flowing black robe and bare feet, the in-house energy healer Stephanie pours blue lotus tea and leads a guided meditation, inviting us to set intentions for our stay. We write our wishes afterwards on scraps of paper and toss the pieces into a fire pit, sending our messages to the spiritual realm. Now it’s 6pm, and the pre-dinner energy is rising in the bar downstairs. I settle instead in the greenhouse for a special three-course dinner curated by ‘wellness chef extraordinaire’ Bettina Campolucci Bordi. To start, there’s a passionfruit cocktail made with Sentia, a non-alcoholic spirit engineered to mimic that first-drink buzz. A plant-powered feast follows: fried courgette flowers filled with macadamia ‘cheese’; whole cauliflower roasted in turmeric; and a spiced-date sticky toffee pudding for dessert – all packed with flavour and a low-key richness.

After dinner, I head to the Penthouse, tonight’s setting for a post-prandial ‘infrared sound bath’. The theory is that red light, which soothes inflammation, can help combat muscle soreness and stimulate the body’s production of melatonin, helping you sleep. And, after an hour lying under a blanket, bathed in a rosy glow, feeling waves of sound from the therapist’s crystal bowls reverberating through my body, I’m sweetly semi-comatose as I head to bed. The serenity doesn’t quite extend to my room, which, while beautifully styled, overlooks the courtyard and bar, still in full DJ-powered swing. But perhaps that’s the lesson: the real spiritual test isn’t transcendence in silence, but remaining calm while the world raves on.

The next morning, a yoga class followed by a virtuous breakfast of poached pear with cashew yoghurt sets me up for deeper immersion at Damu. The spa has its own entrance, opening into a tiny reception area with a curtain concealing what lies beyond. Interest suitably piqued, I'm led into an atrium that doubles as the relaxation area, where sun streams through the skylight onto a central jungle of tropical plants. Beyond are the mud caves, a steamy, earth-coloured warren with what appears to be an altar to mud: a curved counter, ethereally backlit and inset with bowls containing various tones and textures of the stuff. And not just any garden-variety clay, but mineral-rich mud hand-harvested from the Austrian Alps.

The ritual is straightforward: a stint in the steam room to open the pores, a salt scrub to exfoliate, then slather the Alpine mud all over your skin. There are various coloured powders you can mix in to create a bespoke cleansing or brightening face mask. Afterwards, I sit on the heated stone benches to dry. Time disappears, and after rinsing off, my skin feels softer than silk. My scepticism is slipping… Later, I have a superb full-body massage that leaves me spaghetti-limbed, deeply relaxed and feeling utterly attended to, reminding me why pampering, properly delivered, is so addictive.

Finally, it’s time for the showpiece treatment: the Origin Pool, a zero-gravity floatation experience fusing light, sound and vibration therapy. A therapist guides me to a small, circular room, so clinically white that it feels less ‘cosmic journey’ and more ‘awaiting my CT scan’. Once inside, the lights dim and I’m left all alone to lie in the water, shallow as a paddling pool, but packed with Epsom salts, so my body feels weightless as I bob about in the dark. Then the show starts. Multicoloured lights swirl on the ceiling to a soothing soundtrack as choreographed low-frequency vibrations pulse through the water, resonating through my body and creating currents that slowly spin me around. For some people, this would be transcendence – someone more spiritually attuned might even find themselves transported. But for me, it hovered closer to elegant limbo. I missed the decadence of an actual therapist, warm hands, the sense of being looked after rather than simply left to marinate.

When the show ends 45 minutes later, I climb out feeling neither transported nor transformed, just certain that luxury, for me, requires human contact. The Origin Pool may well be a revelation for others, but I’ll choose the massage every time.
