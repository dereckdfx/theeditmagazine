Emerald Fennell, take note: Sense and Sensibility has a romanticism that Wuthering Heights might envy

Esmé Creed-Miles fizzes as Marianne, and Daisy Edgar-Jones is a perfect Elinor in this blockbuster Sense and Sensibility , with a glorious turn from Edit cover star Caitríona Balfe

Por Dora Davies-Evitt · 23 de septiembre de 2026

Are you more of an Elinor or a Marianne? It is the great Austen question: are you sense or sensibility? Headstrong, pragmatic, rational, determined – or romantic, passionate, empathetic and liable to fling yourself hand-on-brow into the nearest meadow? If you still don’t know, Georgia Oakley’s new adaptation of Sense and Sensibility has a fairly foolproof diagnostic: are you more Daisy Edgar-Jones or Esmé Creed-Miles?

The two actresses make the distinction lovely and clear. Edgar-Jones plays Elinor Dashwood with a resolute stare and a convincing commitment to emotional suppression, pressing down her feelings as well as the pleats of her apron and bonnet. Creed-Miles, meanwhile, is positively fizzing as Marianne: eye-rolling, hand-shaking, shrieking, swooning and flashing those electric-blue eyes. It is near impossible to take your eyes off her; her elfin beauty and rakish spirit dominate the screen. There is something witchy about her Marianne – in the genes perhaps, given that her mother, Samantha Morton, played the sorceress Circe so brilliantly in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey this year.

From bidding farewell to time-travel smash Outlander to playing a ‘manifesting’ Mrs Dashwood in Sense and Sensibility, actress Caitríona Balfe is a busy woman. So much so that Dora Davies-Evitt had to hunt her down on holiday at an Irish castle…

Oakley’s film is beautiful, too, shot in a naturally diluted light reminiscent of Joe Wright’s 2005 Pride and Prejudice, with its soft skies and washed-out greens. In this respect, it feels more Brontë than Austen. Marianne does have a great deal of Cathy about her. There is a heightened romanticism here that Emerald Fennell’s Wuthering Heights might well envy.

And yet, for all the windswept yearning, Sense and Sensibility also works as a comedy – which is no bad thing, given that Austen, let’s not forget, is funny. Oakley gets plenty of laughs from the social cruelty of the novel, although occasionally a joke is carried a little too far. Sir John Middleton’s (Turlough Convery) wimpish wife (Chloe Pirrie), for instance, is repeatedly made a mockery of through her fixation on the weather – one time too many.

In honour of Edit cover star Caitríona Balfe's upcoming role as Mrs Dashwood, Jane Austen super-fan Isolde Walters asks: which sister are you most like?

Stacy Martin is suitably ghastly as Fanny Dashwood, while Edit cover star Caitríona Balfe gives Mrs Dashwood a less solemn, more spritely and positive presence than some previous incarnations. Fiona Shaw, meanwhile, is terrific as Mrs Jennings, pitching the character at exactly the right level of annoying. The men – Herbert Nordrum’s Colonel Brandon, George MacKay’s Edward Ferrars and Frank Dillane’s Willoughby – all feel fairly forgettable in the gravitational field of Edgar-Jones and Creed-Miles. Perhaps that is the point.

The shots themselves convey a great deal of waiting… the sisters wait in fields, in chairs, on chaise longues, by windows. They gaze, they sigh; they wait for letters, visits, declarations, inheritances and, ultimately, rescue. It is a rather good Austen analogy: for all the romance, the story is about women being made to sit around until a man decides what is going to happen to them. Don’t sit on going to see it.
