What happened to the most beautiful girl in Britain?

She was tipped for a royal future. Instead, she chose love, hard work and adventure – a life that had everything, until it didn't. Now, after near-ruin and heartbreaking loss, Lady Georgina Bullough is back

Por Charlotte Eagar · 10 de julio de 2026

Lady Georgina Bullough, is talking to me on Zoom, her elfin beauty oddly enhanced by her bobble hat. In her red and sheepskin ski jacket (echoing the gilt-framed ancestor in crimson and ermine behind her), she could be Will Ferrell's love interest in Elf – or Miss Gnome Of the Year. In fact, she is suffering the nightmare of every Perthshire householder: returning from a winter-sun break to find the boiler on the blink. ‘Got back at midnight last night to no heating or hot water. It's 10 degrees in my bedroom,’ she says. ‘I can't even light the wood-burning stove – I haven't had the chimneys swept and they can't come till Monday. Thank God I was only away for three days.’ I suggest going to stay with friends. Or a hotel. Or one of her own rental costumes. ‘I think I need to suffer to provide motivation for change,’ she says with Presbyterian relish. ‘I've made my various beds and I must lie in them.’ Indeed.

Forget Ferrari, there’s another red, Italian set of hot wheels to watch out for

I first met Georgina in the late 1980s at the Skye Balls, those two-day Hebridean rites of passage for Scottish toffs and imported deb-adjacent types where we reeled till 7am in strictly floor-length taffeta and tulle, white tie or kilts, depending on gender (nobody identified as fluid in those days). Georgina was in Bellville Sassoon, ‘the palest blush-pink taffeta’, she says. ‘Everything I had came from Sassoon back then.’

Back then, she was Lady Georgina Murray, the undisputed belle of Scotland: funny, clever and frankly, very grand, brought up in Scone (pronounced ‘scoon’), the ancestral set of the earls of Mansfield. Her father, William, was a particularly impressive earl: clever, rich and glamorous, a QC, a government minister under Mrs Thatcher, then First Crown Estate Commissioner. Scone is definitely at the stateliest end of Scottish statelies – a pink sandstone palace just outside Perth, with Versailles-worthy furniture bought by an ambassadorial ancestor during the French Revolution. The Emperor of Japan stayed when he came to the UK un 1991.

But Georgina's dominating feature was her extraordinary beauty, all golden hair and slanting blue eyes: a Barbara Cartland cover girl. Edit did put her on the front cover in 1993, asking: Is this the most beautiful girl in Britain? ‘So embarrassing. Somebody came up to me at a party , stared at me and said, “No you’re not!” and walked away.’

She touted as a possible bride for Prince Edward in the days when – long before the death of Diana, the Sussexes’ voluntary Montecito exile or the tawdry implosion of the House of York – being a royal bride was considered glamourous.

The wedding of Princess Alexia of Greece and Denmark and Carlos Javier Moraels Quintana saw royals from around the world join Queen Elizabeth II in London – but it almost never happened

‘I’d never even met Prince Edward,’ she says. But everyone assumed she would find a husband who’d be either very grand or very rich indeed. Instead, in 1998, she married, entirely for love, local boy John Bullough of Culcreuch, a Gulf War veteran, scion of respectable department store magnates. A splendid reception for 500 (‘My mother wore gold, her only requirement’) was held at Scone. They moved to Bucharest, part of the Wild East bunch of Scots entrepreneurs – with John Forbes-Leith and John Gray-Cheape – building fortunes from the rubble of Communism. After a decade growing and trading Balkans tobacco, John returned to buy out his family’s business. ‘I had 25 years of adventure, pleasure and joy with John; challenges, yes, but successes too,’ she says.

Challenges? Being good in a crisis is one of Georgina’s attributes. Which is lucky, because for the past 15 years, she has been through enough to make a lot of people reach for the pills.

Three years ago, in May 2023, her husband, clever, kind and handsome John, died out of the blue. We all know the sort of people who keel over in their fifties, but John wasn't one of those. A former Scots Guards officer, he was clean-living, ran five kilometers a day and walked up mountains for fun. He was a special constable; was made a MBE for founding Scotland's Charity Air Ambulance and raising £50 million to keep it flying; and served with the Atholl Highlanders, the UK’s only private army. He was a member of the Royal Company of Archers and formed part of their Honour Guard outside St Giles’, where Queen Elizabeth II’s coffin was viewed by the public before being transported to Westminster Abbey for the state funeral.

The night he died, John was in London to attend meetings for his medical-drone charity, and staying alone in the flat of his sister, Victoria, Countess of Pembroke. She rang Georgina at 10am to say that John had missed three meetings in a row – one with BT’s charitable arm, a potential major donor – and they were worried.

Ruth Ellis escaped an abusive childhood to rise to the top of London's nefarious nightclub scene – but history remembers her as the last British woman to be sentenced to death after she murdered her racing driver lover. Learn the truth behind the tale…

‘I was in the Marks & Spencer car park in Perth,’ said Georgina. ‘My heart sank, I just knew – that is so not John. Your brain starts going through… a thousand things. He just would never not turn up. That’s just inconceivable. My brain was like a tumble dryer. I thought maybe he’d fallen and hit his head in the shower. I started making deals with God.’

But John had had a massive heart attack. Nobody saw it coming. He’d played three sets of tennis the night before.

Some 550 people came to his funeral in St John's Kirk, Perth, the 15th-century church where the teenage Georgina and John had met, one Christmas, in a Jane Austen-approved manner, and were married 10 years later. The grief in the congregation was palpable.

John‘s death came seven years after the couple's near-bankruptcy. He owned McEwens of Perth, a charming department store stocked with Chanel make-up, tweed jackets, Lalique vases and potted shrimps. They also owned branches in Oban and Ballater. But the internet and out-of-town shopping were lapping at its foundations. For 10 years, Georgina and John fought the digital tide. Other department stores – indeed whole industries – drowned around them. Perhaps they should have sold up and bought shares in Amazon instead, but John had a deep loyalty to the shop and staff. Georgina, as well as binging up the couple’s only child, Hercules, was a key part of the McEwens team, running the marketing department, working in the shop.

‘We didn’t just sit in a boardroom. We worked there. We walked the floor. John’s father had a real thing about it. We talked to the staff and the customers. We knew everybody – 120 people worked at McEwens. There was a lot of responsibility,’ Georgina said in an earlier interview in her bright Scottish drawing room, with its 18th-century furniture and epic views.

Many of the staff would come later to John’s funeral. But in 2016, McEwens had to close, and the staff were made redundant as the internet closed over their heads.

On top of this, Georgina and John’s home, Logie House, was nearly swept away, as they’d used it to secure a loan to keep McEwens afloat; it was only saved after a fraught legal battle. It was particularly dreadful because they had built the neo-baronial pile themselves, high above a gorge of the River Almond, on the site of a long-demolished Victorian house owned by Georgina’s father, the 8th Earl of Mansfield. Her life wasn’t supposed to be like this.

Following the death of society grandee Lady Annabel Goldsmith, the family has decided to sell Ormeley Lodge, her home of over 50 years

While AI now seems to be coming for everyone’s jobs, back then Georgina and John felt very alone. ‘There's a sense of shame. But John hadn’t made stupid decisions,’ she says. ‘He was always talking to other, more successful retailers and paying business consultants. I saw what it was doing to someone that I loved very, very much, who didn’t deserve this failure.’

Meanwhile, as well as dealing with the collapse of McEwens and bringing up Hercules, Georgina and John had her aging parents living with them. And Georgina herself had knee and Achilles tendon problems; she had been on crutches for several years and, frankly, like many of the stressed and immobile, grown rather plump.

Georgina’s father died in 2015, her mother in 2022. By then, Georgina and John had rebuilt their lives; John's various charities were thriving. This was their time to be together. Then John died. ‘I don’t remember anything at all of that first year [after his death],’ she says, ‘except moments of great kindness.’ Friends they’d made during their time living in Romania rallied and so, too did Georgina’s Perthshire childhood mates – Charles, Earl of Kinnoull, and the Hon Catherine Drummond-Herdman, the chatelaine of nearby Megginch Castle. Friends tag-teamed to stay with her, coming for two or three weeks. All this went on until Georgina felt she was strong enough to cope.

And then, with 20-year-old Hercules at university in Edinburgh, Georgina was left alone at Logie, on the edge of the gorge, with their three dogs and peacocks.

‘A lot of people went back to their busy lives and were able for periods to forget that John was dead. But I couldn’t forget in any shape or form; his DNA is stamped into the house. He is everywhere. He had great ideas for layout – and his sister is an interior decorator. [And there’s] his weapons collection , which is a very traditional Scottish thing,’ she says of their galleried, two-storey hall festooned with everything from claymores and muskets to a (decommissioned) rocket-propelled grenade between the family portraits. ‘He hung all the paintings. In terms of the grieving process, I suppose it’s been incredibly helpful. Every minute of every day. I am reminded of him and all I have lost.’

She didn’t think of herself until five months later, when she understood she had an obligation to her son to stay alive. ‘I realised I was the only parent he had left. He has no siblings.’ She could reassure him that nothing would happen to her, but how could she truly know?

‘John and I led very similar lives. We both smoked a pack a day until our mid-thirties. We ate the same food. But John was much fitter than me.’ It wasn't just her physical health. ‘I needed to search for my mind, my heart and my body. Because all three were on the brink of collapse.’

For her body, she went to a company called Echelon Health, in London. ‘They tested me for everything. They told me I was pre-diabetic and my BMI was terrible. I thought I still looked fine. The doctor said, “There’s a drug called Ozempic…” You’ll laugh now, but this was in 2023 - early days. I didn’t know anyone else on it. I thought I was cheating. I’m quite Protestant about these things, I thought “I’ve got myself into this, I’ll get myself out.”

Georgina also decided to focus on her mental health. (‘My mental garden’ she calls it.) ‘I realised both my heart and my brain were feeling even more fractured. So I went off social media – Facebook, TikTok, Instagram – completely.’ She listened to long-form podcasts, particularly Dr Benjamin Bikman and Professor Tim Spector on The Diary of a CEO. ‘There’s a huge link between the gut and the brain,’ she says. ‘Tim Spector is the main man worldwide.’

And she returned to reading. ‘I found myself going back to the things from my childhood, where I could try to find strength and meaning.’ She re-read CS Lewis's world of Narnia, post-war children's fantasy stories infused with Christian motifs. ‘Do you remember that place between the worlds in The Magician’s Nephew?’ she asks. ‘There are lots of pools. When the two children jump in, each pool takes them to a different world. It’s a rather green and pleasant place. Lots of trees. You can lie on the grass and think while you choose. It felt like that. It's comforting for anyone who is betwixt and between, I'd left one world behind – my 25 amazing years with John. What’s next? I was pulling myself up out of the bad pool of John’s death to find the next one.’

For her spiritual health, she turned, she says to ancient wisdoms. ‘I was floundering in the absence of my own culture giving me a coherent structure. We’ve lost a lot of that.’

She found herself wearing black, almost by instinct. ‘I wanted to mark this time to myself and to anyone who saw me, without having to explain. It wouldn’t work in Soho, as everyone wears black. but it does in rural Scotland.’

When she was queueing in Tesco to buy her vapes the woman working behind the till leaned over and asked: ‘Who was it?’ ‘I said, “My husband.”’ She said, “Mine died early, too.” She reached across the counter and grasped my hand. We had a tearful moment. Then I bought my vapes and left. It’s the outer expression of what was inside.’

She also took up exercise, buying a Power Plate ‘with the money I saved by not joining the gym at Gleneagles’. She bounces around to cheery songs in her pyjamas all day.

So what now? She’s in almost amniotic state, mulling her options, under the trees, surrounded by pools, in the place between the worlds.

‘That’s quite difficult to answer,’ she says later on WhatsApp. ‘The thought of living solo for the rest of my life is desolate, but when I try to imagine loving someone else, it seems completely bizarre and alien. Maybe that means I’ms not ready yet and possibly never will be. This is the first time in my entire life I have lived just with dogs for company.’

Even growing up, she had a nanny, boarding schools, flatmates in London and then marriage. ‘I can safely say I am not used, suited to or happy being alone! So I live in hope that one day it will feel right to be with someone else. That’s the Murray motto: Spero Meliora – “I hope for better things”. Everyday a new dawn, and every dawn is a new chance of life.’
