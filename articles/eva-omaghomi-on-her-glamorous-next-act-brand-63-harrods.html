From Buckingham Palace to bringing African design to the global stage: Eva Omaghomi on her glamorous next act

Inspired by the experience of dressing for the coronation of King Charles and Queen Camilla, Eva Omaghomi CVO, a former senior advisor to the Royal Household, has entered her next chapter with the launch of Brand63Africa, which arrives at Harrods this week. Style editor Chandler Tregaskes meets her at the boutique…

Por Chandler Tregaskes · 11 de septiembre de 2026

‘It started out as an idea just after the coronation,’ says Eva Omaghomi CVO, the founder of Brand63Africa who spent 17 years as a senior adviser to the Royal Household before launching her luxury social enterprise that aims to bring designers of African heritage to the forefront of a global audience of glamour. ‘I wore an African designer to the coronation, a designer from Ghana, and it took a little while to find something. In the end, I had to contact the designer, get her to make something, and then she got it sent by DHL. It just got me thinking, I don't think it should be that difficult to get hold of luxury designers from Africa and the diaspora…’

600km of style and chic: the French Maison makes Italy its home as it breaks a 14-year hiatus and hits the road via the winding roads of the Dolomites. Style editor Chandler Tregaskes went along for the ride…

That idea soon bore fruit. And now – with a board filled with international talent including chairwoman and former Condé Nast publisher Vanessa Kingori OBE; Chief Diversity & Inclusion Officer at LVMH, Vanessa Moungar; former president of CFDA, Casandra Diggs; and Founder and CEO of Industrie Africa, Nisha Kanabar and more – Omaghomi sits atop of a cohort of African heritage designers who have just this week opened up a pop-up boutique in the world’s most opulent and sought-after department store, Harrods. ‘[Some of the designers] thought it would be an impossibility. A few thought it would be great, but didn’t think we could do it. So, I contacted the British Fashion Council and they connected me to Michael Ward and I came to see [him] about it and Harrods were very open,’ Omaghomi recalls. Clemmie Harris, Head of Buying – Contemporary, Sport and Essentials, Harrods, explains the pop-up has already been a success, adding: ‘It has been particularly rewarding to see customers excited by the opportunity to experience these brands in person, discovering the quality, craftsmanship and creativity behind the labels.’

Networking, community and collaboration are at the beating heart of what Omaghomi does so well. Her five labels under the Brand63Africa umbrella of elegance - Christie Brown, Abiola Olusola , Sukeina, Studio Namnyak, The Cloth - have the most senior figures in their corner helping them to build lasting industry relationships on this side of the globe. But what is she looking for when searching for fashion figures to bring into the fold? ‘It's really important for me to find designers who are very committed to what they do. The craftsmanship's really important [too]. Who they support, who they help to train in terms of their social and community impact on the ground. And those who've got a great story as well, who really know the story behind the fabrics that they're using or the stories that they're telling with different things,’ says Omaghomi.

Citing The Cloth’s use of West Indian Sea Island cotton, the fashion leader praises their ‘intentional’ use behind it. ‘They know why they use it. [The design house] turns 40 this year, so they’re a very established brand. So there was the narrative, the community impact, the craftsmanship, but also the buzz in the particular region about that designer which is quite important,’ says Omaghomi of why she felt inspired to bring them into the network.

As for the other four captivating designers: Christie Brown is ‘amazing with glamour’; Abiola Olusola has ‘beautiful sculptured dresses that are really sexy as well’; Studio Namnyak creates delightfully thoughtful pieces, ‘What appears to look like her tweeds are great work wear’; and she relishes Sukeina for his ‘understanding of the female body coupled with his innovative use of materials and texture, particularly feathers’. ‘We've curated a group of people that are different in every way,’ Omaghomi confirms.

The ‘63’ in her enterprise's moniker, of course, relates to the year that the African Union was formed. It was in 1963 that 32 independent nations created the Organisation of African Unity. Naturally, to put a pulse on what might set the fashion agenda in such a vast, vibrant and all-encompassing area might be difficult to narrow down into the typical ‘big four’ fashion capital’s ephemeral trends, but Omaghomi enthuses that this diversity is what makes African fashion so strong. ‘It’s very much based on craftsmanship, artisans as well. You can mix it with anything,’ she shares.

Circling back to her coronation ensemble, Omaghomi proves this fashion language is international. ‘I was wearing an outfit by a designer from Ghana, an Aspinal of London handbag and Manolo Blahnik shoes. You can mix it in with anything. So I think I would say that craftsmanship and the story behind it is really important. Heritage is really important. Culture is really important. But I think as people go to find designers of African heritage or descent that they like, they should try and look for something that's in their own style as well.’

As for those budding designers looking to learn from Omaghomi’s illustrious career and her verdant network of international creatives, she shares the following: ‘Lean into the community and heritage. I like to use this phrase, “You might as well be yourself and be proud of that”’. ‘I think, a lot of the time, certainly the way I was raised, you were raised down a particular path of what creativity is. And I think I would encourage them to open their minds even more because what I found with all of these designers is they've gone off the beaten track and gone beyond what makes for the typical aesthetic of what they've been taught that they should make. And so I would say, definitely looking from influences around the world and using those influences in your work, building on your own cultural heritage, but don't be afraid to use influences from around the world as well. Whatever speaks to you.’
