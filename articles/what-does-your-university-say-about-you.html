What does your university say about you?

‘Brookes, not books’? Old hat. ‘Dullham’? Forget about it. This year's cohort of freshers has a brave new world of university stereotypes and social scenes to contend with. From the best libraries for nursing a hangover to holding a conversation about an Old Etonian's latest Land Economy lecture, brush up on Edit 's ultimate guide to uni life.

Por Edit · 25 de septiembre de 2026

The academic season has rolled around once more – and what is studenthood without the enduring power of the cliché? Finding yourself – and your social tribe – is a tall ask, and for some, the performance of a lifetime. Gone are the days of Bullingdon bad behaviour: here is the definitive guide to the stereotypes, the places to see and be seen, and how to skip town. Lo and behold, here is Edit’s taxonomy of Britain's most glittering institutions and budding bright young things. Did yours make the cut?

Ask not what going to Oxford says about you, but what you can say about going to Oxford. Because Lord knows you love to talk about it. Save the faux-humility (‘Oh, I was in Oxford for uni’), no one is assuming you’re a genius – especially not the Bridge bouncers. Was it like Brideshead? No. Sure, you might have danced with the Princess of Belgium, or Malala, but you probably spent most of your time in the prison-like Gladstone Link, hungover in the Social Sciences Library, or feigning active listening techniques mid tute with a dead-eyed Old Etonian. If there’s one thing studying at Oxford will teach you, it’s that ‘clever’ and ‘dumb’ are far from mutually exclusive.

You’ve prepared to abandon any dreams of academia. For the revellers, Tudor Hall girls, and Teddies boys who clipped entry on the last legs of their academic careers: Newcastle is refreshingly unserious – and actually fun. You’re probably a member of the extended Scottish aristocracy, who selected degrees based on a sub-three hour drive from your estate (they prefer to disgrace themselves at Trebles bars across the border). You spend half of your time at this so-called Russell Group Uni on an LNER train venturing into Northumberland with a crop of Oundeilians keen to show you the real Saltburns of the north, or flitting between Kings Road-esque cafes in Jesmond (all Matcha served in this student ville is ceremonial-grade, after all). Brush up on your land economy: these denizens are very concerned about the farmer’s tax.

Silence, naysayers – the ‘Dullham’ era is (perhaps) over. Actual Oxbridge rejects are few and far between at Durham – the gap-yah capital is the height of your ambition. You enjoy the presumption of being an Oxbridge reject: but you wouldn’t have dared apply. Your college is your king: which means that the milieu is not a far cry from your fellow student’s former boarding schools. You’ll wince at the Hatfield Schoffel-donners, but you’ll eventually carve out your own little niche of posh-core: rugby matches, reeling balls and referring to 21st birthday parties as ‘21st season’ are the social-musts to get your head around – bonus points if you’ve pubbed with a Hooray Henry barking on about ‘positive discrimination’ at Oxbridge. You are somewhat adaptable: certainly to a robust tolerance (or sheer willpower) for alcohol to withstand the local nightlife. When partying at Jimmy’s is overdone, you’ll brave the nighttime LNER to Newcastle to try and keep up with your hardcore friends – only to protest at the lack of intelligence among the Jesmond crowd (but be honest, Hugo; we know you’re actually studying land science). Back in snuggly, sleepy Durham, you’ll learn to take your Guinness very seriously – you might even find yourself wanting a “proper” Guinness at The Swan from Becky the barmaid (she will neither care nor learn your name). Get on your Mrs Dalloway grind: this university town is the Dinner Party capital in the country.

This home-counties haven is for the sweeter folk: you’re mildly more academic than your Newcastle pals, but you’re certainly not a scholar. As a person, you like to keep things steady, ‘showing face’ is your social order: you’re obsessively ‘having fun’ in efforts to level with fellow Russell-Groupers, but you’re not an afters kind of person, preferring an Uber home at 2am and peppermint tea in bed after a sweaty night at Teepee. ‘Mate’ is a high-frequency word in your vocabulary – you’re certainly not looking to expand your vernacular. That being said, be sure to refer to everyone by their surname (however clunky), and fret not when the person in question doesn’t respond. The extended SW6 bubble is a comfortable place for you to be – you’d probably consider Soho East London, and sneer at the idea of living in Clapham.

Heavy on the ‘I didn’t develop a personality separate from my uni until my mid 20s’ because when you’ve nailed yourself into the smart set, you think of little else! Thespdom is a shuddering thought for you – you’d hate to be so predictable as to join the Cambridge Footlights (alumnae dub this sub-set of Cambridge an ‘evil breed’). You possess, quite proudly, an incomparable superiority complex: because you went to the liberal, less predictable of the two leading institutions (the narrow margin between Oxford and Cambridge’s rankings is very important to you). You’re hard-bred: work and play co-exist neatly in your lifestyle – being busy is not a personality trait for you. You’ll power through burnout like nothing else, but you’re fiercely independent – and frankly terrifying. A blunt ‘Cambridge’ is apt when asked where you go to university – no pussyfooting around.

‘Welcome to Leeds, what can I get you, a bag of methadone or some dub to step to?’ rapped old Harrovian MC Slick Nick in the 2010s. And here we have the very essence of Leeds during that period, let’s call it its heyday. There were Marlburians brought up in the home counties with thick – heavily affected – cockney accents alongside a fleet of Downe House and Queen Margaret’s girls who congregated in the Boddington canteen at 5.30pm every evening to line their stomachs (and plan the succeeding evening’s pre-lash location). Depending on your ‘flavour’ the biggest nights were Tuesdays at Mint Club for ‘Bigger than Barry’, a dubstep night, attracting wannabe DJs; Wednesdays at Gatecrasher for the muscled ‘sport bois’ (who definitely were not at Barry’s the night before) or Thursdays at Mez Club for Tequila where ‘Tequila boy’ Andy Jordan – soon to appear on Made in Chelsea, opposite fellow Leeds alumni Jamie Laing and Sam Cussins – would dole out shots. History of Art girlies prefer the beautiful Brotherton library – a place to see and be seen – whereas any kind of hangover called for Edward Boyle, the Brutalist library where you could disappear into a crevice and never be seen. The best afterparty was always at Crispies in Hyde Park. Slice of pizza and chips for £1? Sold.

These two lots are roped together because, in reality, there’s little difference between them for you – and anyone else. You’re an intense party person, loves to be out – but the Streetonian within you is hard to control at times (you’re self-aware about your privilege but in the worst way). You have no interest in socialising beyond your group: none of your university friends had less than ninety mutual friends with you on Instagram before you met them. Your home is either Gloucestershire, Hampshire, or the quiet hills of Somerset – and you think you’re a well-ingrained Londoner because your second home is Shepherd’s Bush, not Notting Hill. Moderate intelligence is your bag – it never occurred to you to apply anywhere else because events are your passion, but you certainly can’t do them sober. Restaurants in Clifton or sourdough bakeries in Fallowfield are your guilty pleasure.

You’re most likely a Romantic, who once feigned an interest in Oxbridge but ultimately couldn’t push yourself the final mile. Filtering in from the upper echelons of British Public Schools, you like being institutionalised, and so slotting into Pollock was a nice adjustment. You’re English-Scottish (and very keen to let everyone know) and either you’re doing a random degree because the stunning baroque of New Town stole your eye, or you actually tried for Oxbridge, but probably didn’t get to the interview stage. You’re too scared of partying for Manchester or Bristol: you’re equally mesmerised by cool Glasgow students but it wouldn't occur to you to go for a night out there. Everything in your wardrobe is Brora (a skinny scarf is your accessory of choice) and a candle in a wine bottle is the height of sophistication in your opinion. You’re there to flick your hair back in Paris on that high-coveted year abroad (secondary personality trait incoming). Be sure to tell everyone that your fourth year is a Master’s and it’s essential because ‘it’s nice to have everyone back together again’ like your social group is a splintering family.

Results day has the next gen in a furore. If you're heading to Cambridge, it's time to get au fait with what your college of choice says about you

Yes, you did get through to a final-round Oxford interview, but it doesn't matter that you didn’t make the cut: you’re happier in Fife. The summit of your academia would have been a coup for your parents, but things have definitely worked out in your favour. You’re a hard working type and great conversion at a dinner party. You’re quietly from an upper-crust public school: but appropriately humble when asked where you went to school (though less humble when you will openly comment on how many Americans there are in Fife). Finding a fellow St Andrew’s partner is terribly important, as is the hour that you left the Welly Ball last year (anything less than 5am is a failure). Your favourite joke is to say that your university is a ‘cult’ and it never occurs to you that the joke is old. Your Instagram is wholesome and you don’t bother going home for Reading week because London is ‘too busy’ and the golf is too good. You’ll likely buy a Defender the year you leave University.

A noble mention, because you’re not a university student, nor do you ever intend to use your degree. You’re riotous good fun, and don’t mind being the butt of the joke (‘Brookes not books’ doesn’t phase you anymore). You’re either never in Oxford, or you’re there too much. All of your allowance is spent on Chiltern Railways to fund your weekend trips up to London, or on petrol, to escape to your housemate’s palatial estate in the North Cotswolds. Aristos are by the dozen – your mum definitely schedules your Ocado orders for you, and you have Daylesford chicken broth in your university kitchen’s freezer.

You’re likely an Oxbridge reject who booked the next BA flight to Dublin to save your blushes – or flirted with the idea of Edinburgh but wanted to be different. Sally Rooney’s kingdom can be divided into duchies: the international students (among them very well dressed American girls who flock en masse in search of soft-spoken counterparts worthy of Normal People), the bohemians, and the Old Etonians role-playing with the republic. An unscratchable individuality complex is essential (bonus points for anyone at a Sloaney pub that tells you they go to an ‘international university’), as are your own decks (a Christmas present from mummy), hoop earrings (or a medically infected cartilage piercing), and a keenness to perfect ‘splitting the G’ (when the line between the dark stout and the creamy white head lands right in the middle of the ‘G’ logo on the glass) – as a locale. Within two months, you’ll be sighing at the tourists queuing for a frothy pint at Temple bar. And never forget how important you feel boarding a plane to return home – or the stress to pack your university room into suitcases for a plane: it’s an Odyssean quest Homer could not have penned.

The university scene in London is a tale of two – or five – cities: all under the same umbrella, rotating the same pubs, but never breaking bread. If you’ve wrangled a spot at Imperial College, you delight in the shock-factor of your intelligence: you’re either affable enough to offset your intense brainpower for social purposes (has friends) or on a such high dosage of ADHD medication that it’s recreational (doesn’t have friends). Shocking either way. At UCL or King’s College, you’re so metropolitan that it would kill you not to be able to go to hot small-plates restaurant openings in London and you were too worried about coffee shops outside of the capital not serving almond milk, so you stuck to London. If you’re at the Courtauld, you’ll remind everyone of a retired Conservative politician under Margaret Thatcher, and say that £100 per head for a table at Scotch is a ‘bargain.’ You’re part of a crop of disgraced public schoolboys, and anything less than History of Art at Cambridge was an embarrassment for you, and your family – so the Courtauld was the nonchalant (lesser) option. If you’re a Goldsmith-goer, you’re impossibly cool – you’re also at liberty to put down any neophyte who hasn’t heard of the artist you discovered two days ago, because that is your artistic prerogative. You relish in the fact that you live in Bermondsey - but you’ll sneak back to Notting Hill when the heating bill is out of your control.
