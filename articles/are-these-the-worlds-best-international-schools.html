Schools

Are these the world's best international schools?

What's in a name? That's the question prospective parents might ask of the sister sites to some of the UK's best schools popping up across the world. Edit 's education editor jets to the Middle and Far East to discover whether these branded schools truly are A*

Por Tori Cadogan · 28 de agosto de 2026

An interview with Britain's Strictest Head, a tour of international schools and a celebration of 100 years of the Royal Ballet School: Edit's education editor, Tori Cadogan, reveals what to expect from this year's guide, on sale 3rd September with the October issue

‘What sets the British international schools apart is not simply their heritage, but the rigour with which that is protected,’ our High Commissioner to Singapore, Nikesh Mehta, tells me. ‘Strong links to their founding UK schools – from governance and leadership to curriculum and inspection – ensure that the highest standards are upheld.’ But rumours have always abounded that some of our branded international establishments have higher standards than others. So I set out to visit four of our best recognised, most established – and most active in franchising – at their flagship outposts. Although I know their originals well, I also visited them again just before setting off to chat with their heads and the CEOs of their international schools (all co-ed) and ensure they were fresh in my mind. The journey took me from Brighton to Dubai, Rugby to Thailand, Harrow to Hong Kong and, finally, Dulwich to Singapore.

Harrow Hong Kong

If you’re flying into Hong Kong, keep your eyes peeled. As Mel Mrowiec, founding head of Harrow International School Hong Kong and former deputy head of Harrow UK explains, you may just glimpse a ‘great, white semicircular structure, based on the Royal Crescent in Bath. An architect firm with a British heritage designed it as a big statement, and it certainly feels very British. It gives a sense of the strong atmosphere here right from the very beginning.’

The Harrow Hong Kong building – perched in the Gold Coast Tuen Mun district – is iconic. How fitting for the third foreign outpost of one of the UK’s most erudite institutions. While the original school was founded in 1572 under a royal charter from Queen Elizabeth I, Harrow Hong Kong opened in 2012, with an impressive starting intake of 750 children aged from three to 18 years old, and a promise to offer families the eminence of education available at its counterpart 6,000 miles away. Now with 1,600 pupils and waiting lists for nearly every year group, Harrow Hong Kong can only be viewed as a genuine success story.

As can its Palladian-columned building, whose scale is truly awe-inspiring. The school looks out on its 4G Astroturf pitch, then beyond to the sea. On the 14-acre campus, pupils play rugby and football, fence and swim in a 25-metre indoor pool. Next to the latter is the Queen Elizabeth II Hall where pupils gather on Monday mornings for assembly – their straw hats offering a visual connection to their heritage – before commencing their studies. When they do, the music teaching, for example, is exceptional: there’s an entire corridor where pupils learn jazz and rap, and practise for the symphony orchestra. Meanwhile, the academics are stellar, with 70 per cent achieving A*–A at A-level, and 93 of last year’s 119 leavers heading off to the UK’s G5 universities (Oxford, Cambridge, LSE, UCL, Imperial). Now that it has put down roots, Harrow Hong Kong has become a true fusion of cultures, taking the best of British and combining it with that of its locale. Head Rosie McColl says she loves it every week ‘when the dragon-dance team walks past my window’. But underpinning everything is ‘the real belief that a well-rounded education involves everyone doing sports and music and, of course, leadership. And this comes from Harrow UK.’

When the school first opened in Hong Kong’s New Territories, it had to take a leap of faith, as most of its prospective families lived in the Greater Bay area: a considerable commute. Now the location of its sprawling campus, with direct access to the famed Maclehose Trail – a 100-kilometre hiking route – and its proximity to the beach, seems an extraordinary piece of luck for the Harrow Schools Group.

Since its first international opening, in Bangkok in 1998, the latter has become ‘the biggest British branded school group,’ says Alastair Land, head of Harrow UK. ‘So why is that really significant? Not because it is nice to be big, but because the money that comes from the [affiliation] licences means we can support British bursary holders. That’s the reason for doing it. So that boys who couldn’t otherwise enjoy a Harrow education can.’ The license income from its family of international schools is in the range of £6 million to £7 million a year.

With 16 schools – and three more due to open in the next two years – there’s a real focus at home on maintaining Harrow’s standards around the world. As Land explains: ‘If you’re going to wear that word, it’s got to mean something. For me, it is extraordinarily exhilarating to go into our international schools; and it could be anyone from Reception age upwards, wearing a straw hat, walking past me and “capping” me [touching their brims in traditional salutation] because the Harrow-ness means that much.’

For McColl, what binds the schools is not just the concentration on such key Harrow values as honour, courage and humility; but the focus on service. She elaborates: ‘When the Tai Po fires broke out in November last year, the students were immediately asking: “What are we doing? We want to help.” It’s part of the DNA here, and it is something I find very special.’ Much like the school itself.

Brighton College Dubai

When I visited Dubai in late 2025, the focus was on the flow of British families moving to the UAE for a sunnier, safer, tax-free life. However, the Iranian war has brought into sharp focus how febrile this area is. As I write, a ceasefire is in place; significantly, pupils are back at school and families at Brighton College Dubai are full of praise for its compassionate guidance during a difficult time, as well as their fantastic online teaching.

It’s led by Simon Crane, who’s evangelical about life in Dubai and whose dedication to education in the UAE has been rewarded with a Golden Visa. While 60 per cent of the students are from the UK and 37 per cent from 77 other nations, the local contingent amounts to only three per cent. Nonetheless, Crane is deeply respectful of the latter’s customs. Every Friday, school finishes at midday so that Muslim pupils can be home for prayers by 1pm. Due to the heat, the day often starts at 6.40am for extracurricular clubs, from which there are more than 200 to choose, and finishes at 3.20pm, with after-school clubs running until 4.20pm. During Ramadan, children only attend school in the mornings.

One mother says, ‘Of particular note are the girls here. At their age it can be a bumpy ride, but we’ve found the girls at Brighton to be kind, welcoming, inclusive and not cliquey. They seem to be a little more traditional in their values. There’s more emphasis on sport and family than at other UAE schools, where a lot of time is spent on social media and hanging out with boys at the mall!’ Like all Brighton Colleges, Dubai’s has a certain energy. Everyone says hello as I am given a tour by the head girl Tanaiya and head boy Reihan.

Inside, there is a big central atrium, housing a neon-signed Brighton Coffee shop where parents gather at drop-off before playing padel on the school’s courts. And in keeping with the original’s long-held reputation for pastoral care, the Dubai branch runs a programme overseen by Dr Lené Janse Van Rensburg (who has both an MA in play therapy and a PhD in psychology). She introduces me to three dynamic Sixth-formers, who dress me in a superhero outfit – a red cape and mask – and lead me into a room of Year 5 pupils, where they conduct a masterclass on emotions. It is engaging, informative and brilliant; I chide myself for feeling a little self-conscious in my outfit…It’s outside that the benefits of being in Dubai really come to the fore. There’s a fabulous outdoor pool with plenty of shade; an enormous sports field with a running track; and, best of all, an outdoor theatre, where I watch a modern-day adaptation of Romeo and Juliet. Back in Britain, Leah Hamblett says: ‘I feel like, if you cut through our internationals, they have got Brighton College running through their veins.’ And having visited, I couldn’t agree more.

Rugby School Thailand

Rugby School Thailand is bucking that trend. That’s largely thanks to its owners, the Teepsuwans.Co-founder Taya ‘Eve’ Teepsuwan, who is a former deputy governor of Bangkok, has education in her blood: her mother is the famed Khunying (Lady) Sasima Srivikorn, who in 1963 founded Srivikorn School – now St Andrew’s – and her husband is Nataphol Teepsuwan, a former minister of education. The family dedicated an 80-acre plot of land in Chonburi to the school; the result is the largest campus in the country, its scale and scope are something to behold.

Near capacity at 1,200 pupils – 200 of whom are boarders – the school hopes to reach 1,500. The children start at two years old and stay until they are 18. The long school day (8am to 6pm) ensures everyone gets involved in the multitude of opportunities on offer. And crucially, Rugby UK’s ‘The Whole Person, The Whole Point’ programme is woven into the Thai outpost’s DNA. As Eve Teepsuwan explains, ‘The “Whole Person” in the sense that education is not just about academic results, although they are still very important. But we help develop their core values, ensure they find a passion and reach their maximum potential.’ Still, Grindlay says it’s essential that Thai culture is embraced. ‘We’ve got to have that local flavour. We’re much more relaxed, much more party, much more Thai.’ He fondly remembers his school’s first big football tournament: ‘I’d never seen anything like it. Parents turned up with pictures of their children on sticks and balloons. We had music blasting, hotdog machines and popcorn. It was so fun.’

What’s really luring parents is the facilities – alongside the stellar academics, wraparound support and happy teachers. The pre-prep has an outside play area accessed by a giant slide from the classrooms above. It has its own shaded swimming pool and forest school with a family of giant tortoises. The pre-prep also shares its Learner Profile with Bilton Grange prep, part of the Rugby UK family, so the children here can also be Creative Chameleons, Persevering Penguins or Caring Koalas.

Boarding is offered to Years 6 to 8 in Marshall House, whose cosy and relaxed atmosphere – complete with hammocks and bean bags in its common rooms – has attracted a hitherto tentative market. But Eve Teepsuwan is evangelical in the face of any reticence. Having put her own children through Brambletye Prep, she says, ‘We really believe in the UK boarding system because we saw it transform our kids. They came from a very luxurious lifestyle here: having nannies doing everything for them, stuck in the traffic almost every day, doing a lot of tutoring. At Brambletye, we saw them becoming more independent, with good communication skills and having more discipline.’

Older boarders are in houses whose names would be familiar to their British counterparts (Rupert Brooke and School House). By the same token, the prefects are known as the Levée; and academically, its students compare favourably with their peers in this country, with 51 per cent scoring A*–A at A-level. As for the choice of subjects, it’s phenomenal, with many pupils taking four A-levels and completing an EPQ before heading to all corners of the world for university.

The sports provision is world-class: ranging from the palatial Webb Ellis Centre to the 50-metre competition pool (and 25-metre training pool); from the water sports lake to the seemingly endless playing fields. The arts are nurtured, too; hence a 750-seat auditorium is under construction, scheduled to open this year.

In a culture where tutoring to excess is the norm, this is a true alternative. This establishment gives its children all the academic support they require within school hours; so when their day finishes, they’re free to expand their other interests or spend time with their families. Eve concludes: ‘I feel so proud. If you can change one person’s life, it is very important and valuable.’ And through Rugby School Thailand, ‘we get the opportunity to change thousands of students’ lives.’

Dulwich College Singapore

Anyone familiar with Dulwich College UK’s stunning red-brick, Grade II-listed Barry Buildings (completed in 1866) could be forgiven for taking a second or even third glance on arriving at the college’s Singapore campus. For with intricate care and absolute precision, the historic façade has been meticulously recreated. And this care, respect for tradition and attention to detail is a perfect metaphor for the pedagogic hive within.

Nearly at full capacity with 3,000 pupils, its scale and energy are something to behold. Opened in 2014 on a site that was previously jungle, the 12-acre Butik Batok campus has seen a whole residential neighbourhood grow around it. But as David Ingram, head of Dulwich College Singapore, notes: for such a big school community, ‘we’ve got small-school relationships, and that is really tangible in lots of different ways’. Children can start here at two years old in the ‘Ducks’ space, whose enormous outside area includes a butterfly garden. It contains six nursery classes of around 16 pupils, each with three teachers caring for them.

The junior school, which takes children from the ages of seven to 11, follows the English National Curriculum, modified for geographical context. (Despite strict government regulation – even stricter than China’s – Ingram says it’s ‘really broad, and internationally minded’.) And they do so in style, their learning areas dotted with meditative koi carp ponds, plenty of greenery and such touches of British heritage as a bright red phone box. Then it’s on to senior school for iGCSEs before progressing to the IB. Post-16 pathways have expanded to include A-levels, for those who want to take STEAM pathways, and the Dulwich High School Diploma. Results are excellent; on my visit, I see the senior school’s head Melanie Ellis proudly bearing a certificate from Pearson Edexcel proclaiming that four pupils had achieved the highest marks in the 2025 Outstanding Learner Awards in Singapore, another the highest in Asia, and two the highest in the world.

My visit corresponds with the final days of the Christmas term and the excitement is palpable. Outside on the Astroturf is the most frenetic and well-supported sports day conceivable, with pupils racing, lots of cheering, loud music blaring and parents spilling from the Clocktower Café. Inside is no less busy, with preparations taking place for a concert in the 742-seat Alleyn Theatre. One can only imagine the energy on-site for the Dulwich Olympiad, when 13 Dulwich international schools converge for sport, drama, music, art and design.

But as Instagram notes, ‘We come from a very privileged community, and parents are not only focused on making a difference, but on the ideas of responsibility and respect, and using your privilege for good.’ This is reflected not just in the pupils heading into the local community to help, but also in the school’s sustainability aims. And nowhere is that more evident than in the Greenhouse. Completed in November 2023, this net-zero, seven-storey learning lab contains a 400-seat auditorium, a professional teaching kitchen, film and media suites, plenty of Sixth-form workspaces and – best of all, according to one Year 6 student – kinetic tiling, which converts movement into energy. ‘We can jump on the tiles and challenge other houses,’ they say. Another says, ‘We love that the Greenhouse is carbon neutral and that we can make things out of recycled bottle tops. There are trees in the building and 290 solar panels.’

As my visit comes to a close, we stop at the seven-station dining hall – which includes Wok Hei Chinese, Quench salad bar (with the most delicious mango smoothies), Spice it Up Indian, Oishi Japanese, a western delicatessen and fired-up ovens producing pizzas – reflecting DCSG’s truly international scope.

As British High Commissioner Nikesh Mehta says: ‘In Singapore, these schools play a particularly important role, contributing to one of the world’s most competitive education ecosystems while enriching its international and local communities through academic excellence, character education and a genuinely global outlook.’ With Singapore offering such a clean, safe community in which to raise your family, Ingram says: ‘You can put your child through boarding school in the UK and it’s about the same price; but the locals choose here.’

Mehta describes one family who opted to send their children to the UK only to look back with regret, ‘because they saw their children’s friends who had stayed had got amazing results and gone on to amazing universities, and that their international-mindedness and the way in which they looked at the world was just so much broader and richer than their son and daughter’s peer sets’. With a Singaporean passport currently considered the most precious in the world, an education here may be of equal value.
