Inside Trent Park, the grand house with a wartime secret

Once a glittering society retreat and later the scene of audacious wartime espionage, Trent Park is ready for its big reveal. The Marquess of Cholmondeley gives Edit a tour

By Dora Davies-Evitt · October 6, 2026

It’s a bright summer morning at Trent Park, the grand house hidden behind the suburban neatness of Cockfosters. Fresh from an almost decade-long restoration, the mansion is entering a new chapter. Edit has been invited for a first look, touring the property with David Cholmondeley, 7th Marquess of Cholmondeley, the great-nephew of Trent Park’s former owner Sir Philip Sassoon.

A confection of Georgian origins and Jazz Age glamour, it sits among rolling parkland so beautiful that it hardly feels like north London. There are lakes, terraces and ancient trees. The grass falls away towards the horizon. Somewhere nearby, commuters are making their way down the Piccadilly line.

Yet Trent Park is more than a handsome country house. Today it occupies a curious place in Britain’s historical imagination. Few buildings can claim such a remarkable dual identity. It is simultaneously a stately home – once the seat of the storied Sassoon family – and the setting for one of the most sophisticated intelligence operations of the Second World War.

Sir Philip Sassoon transformed this grand estate on London’s uppermost edge into arguably England’s most glamorous country house during the 1920s and 1930s. Royalty, politicians, artists, socialites and Hollywood stars streamed through its doors. Winston Churchill stayed frequently; Edward, the Prince of Wales, visited with Wallis Simpson; the young Princess Elizabeth played in its halls; Charlie Chaplin and Fred Astaire passed through. But by the end of the decade, German generals would unwittingly reveal their military secrets within its walls, their conversations recorded in an audacious British espionage operation.

Post-war, the house had a long stint as a college and university campus until 2013, after which the site was abandoned and fell into disrepair. While touring the estate for unrelated reasons, Jason Charalambous, a former local councillor, was struck by the sad neglect of a once-grand house. Its dilapidated state prompted him to launch an ambitious restoration project that would ultimately save it. More than 10 years later, Trent Park has been reborn: the ground floor and basement now house a museum, while the upper floors have been transformed into luxury apartments.

Waiting at the entrance is Cholmondeley. As co-chair of the Trent Park Museum Trust with Charalambous, he has spent the past year helping oversee the restoration of the house. Tall and courteous, dressed in brown loafers and a cashmere jumper, he is in jovial spirits, keen to show everything he and his team have achieved. (There is something deliciously fun about being shown around one of Britain’s most extraordinary wartime intelligence sites by a marquess whose family once lived here.)

Standing in one of the restored reception rooms, it is easy to see Trent Park’s great attraction. The restoration has been painstaking to the point of obsession. Original colour schemes have been rediscovered under decades of paint. Paintings that once hung here are being recreated from old photographs. Furniture is returning from storage. Cholmondeley points to walls whose colours are the result of conservators scraping through layers of decoration. ‘We’ve pretty much put back the original colour scheme,’ he says in his soft, clipped accent. ‘We scraped away and found the colours that were here.’ It quickly becomes apparent just how vast his knowledge is on the subject. Elsewhere, curtains have been recreated from archive photos. Original oak floors have been relaid and period panelling preserved. Chinese wallpaper has been reproduced from examples in museum collections. Even the forecourt has been revived, revealing flagstones sourced from Westminster Bridge and a Union Jack design that was concealed during the war to avoid creating an obvious aerial landmark for Luftwaffe pilots.

In the library, an eccentric decorative flourish is being reinstated: a recreation of Horace Walpole’s handwritten description of the famous painting said to depict the first pineapple grown in England. The text is being carefully painted on the wall as we look around. ‘Lacquer was his thing,’ Cholmondeley says of his great-uncle’s taste in interiors.

Today Sassoon’s name is less familiar than perhaps it should be. In his lifetime, however, he occupied the positions of politician, collector, aesthete and formidable host. Descended from two of the great Jewish dynasties of Europe – the Sassoons and the Rothschilds – he was among the wealthiest young men in Britain. Educated at Eton and Christ Church, Oxford, he entered politics but made society his true stage. Guests drifted through his homes like courtiers might orbit royal palaces: Noël Coward, George Bernard Shaw and Lawrence of Arabia were among those who mingled with royalty and the political elite at his house parties.

The Sassoon family became connected to the Cholmondeley family in August 1913 when Sybil Sassoon (Cholmondeley’s grandmother) married the Earl of Rocksavage, who later became the 5th Marquess of Cholmondeley. Sybil was something of a muse to the artist John Singer Sargent and the subject of several of his portraits. The Cholmondeley family’s principal seat is Houghton Hall in Norfolk, the Palladian mansion built for Britain’s first prime minister, Sir Robert Walpole, in the 1720s. Walking through Trent Park, traces of those wider family connections appear everywhere. Paintings and furniture that once belonged to Sassoon have returned from Houghton Hall, where many pieces were stored after the war. In a sense, the restoration has reunited two branches of the same family story.

Painstakingly restored, Trent Park is almost like a house waiting for its owner to return from lunch, apart from the Blue Room, a former haunt of Churchill and now a bustling cafe. It doesn’t take much imagination to picture the statesman here again, settling into an armchair, cigar in hand, exchanging gossip with his host.

Cholmondeley leads me past a portrait of Sir Richard Jebb, the physician who first built the house. ‘This is the original owner,’ he tells me. ‘He was the doctor to King George III.’ After successfully treating the king’s brother, the Duke of Gloucester, during a severe illness in Trento, Italy, Jebb was rewarded with the lease of Trent Park. (The estate dates back to the 14th century, when it formed part of one of Henry IV’s hunting grounds.)

Sir Philip Sassoon inherited Trent Park from his father, Sir Edward Sassoon, who purchased the lease in 1909. Philip maintained his country house at Port Lympne in Kent and his London residence on Park Lane. The three properties formed a glittering social triangle through which the political and cultural elite of the age circulated. Besides hosting, Sassoon twice servedas undersecretary of state for air and became one of the most enthusiastic advocates of British aviation during the interwar years. Visitors to Trent Park might have found themselves discussing aviation policy over lunch before watching Charlie Chaplin entertain after dinner. Sassoon himself was known for staging elaborate themed exhibitions dedicated to different periods ofdecorative arts, particularly at his Park Lane residence. He was also a great letter writer, according to Cholmondeley, who has read correspondence between his grandmother and Sassoon. ‘It’swonderful to have such great evidence of their close relationship,’ he says.

For all the famous visitors, however, the most remarkable chapter in Trent Park’s history was still to come. Sassoon died suddenly in 1939, aged only 50, following complications from influenza. Then, within months, Britain was at war.

From servant’s daughter to ‘collector of kings’, there was no one like Margaret Greville, whose dazzling diamonds have adorned royal women for over 100 years. On the anniversary of her death, discover the preeminent name in regal jewellery, whose legacy lives on through the likes of Queen Camilla and the Princess of Wales

Like many great houses across the country, Trent Park became part of the war effort. Unlike almost all of them, however, it became a luxury prison for senior German officers. At first, the house received captured Luftwaffe pilots. Then came German generals and senior officers.

‘It’s such a fantastic story,’ says Cholmondeley. Behind the elegant plasterwork, in the state rooms and even outside in the gardens, secret microphones had been hidden in extraordinary numbers. They lurked in walls, skirting boards, light fittings and furniture. Benches, trees and plant pots were bugged. A number of senior German prisoners, including 59 generals, passed through the house without realising that almost every word they spoke was being recorded.

Below their feet sat teams of listeners, many of them Jewish refugees who had fled Nazi persecution and now found themselves eavesdropping on Hitler’s officers. They had been recruited precisely because their German was modern enough to understand the day’s contemporary slang and military jargon; an offhand joke or casual remark could contain intelligence of enormous significance.

The operation was the brainchild of intelligence officer Thomas Kendrick and remains one of the least celebrated triumphs of the war. While Bletchley Park became a national legend, Trent Park disappeared into official secrecy.

The deception went astonishingly far. The prisoners were encouraged to live comfortably. They dined well and walked in the grounds. They attended football matches. They smoked, drank and socialised. Some were taken into London to dine at The Ritz. ‘They were encouraged to have a normal life, within reason,’ says Cholmondeley. ‘They would talk, and every word was recorded.’

Every effort was made to make them comfortable enough not to suspect a thing. Staff appeared to be ordinary domestic servants. Information was carefully managed.

On one occasion, a newspaper, partially faked with a story about German rocket testing, was left out – its discussion by generals helped to uncover the Nazi Vengeance Weapons programme.The entire environment was designed to lower their guard. In many respects, it resembled a wartime version of The Truman Show. The prisoners inhabited a world designed for them by unseen directors.

Among the German-speaking Jewish refugees drafted as secret listeners at Trent Park was Ernst Lederer, whose granddaughter is the actress and comedian Helen Lederer – best known for her role in Absolutely Fabulous and today a trustee of the museum. The listeners – about 100 of them – were tasked with identifying dialects, decoding military slang and transcribing conversations.

Conversations they captured revealed details of military technology, battlefield conditions and strategic thinking – all essential to the victory of the Allied forces and the defeat of Nazism in Europe. In addition, some prisoners unknowingly discussed atrocities committed across occupied Europe, giving British intelligence early evidence of war crimes and mass murder.

For decades almost nobody knew the story of Trent Park; the files remained classified until 1996. That fact may explain why Trent Park still lacks the mythology attached to Bletchley Park. Surely its remarkable wartime activity is crying out for a Quentin Tarantino adaptation. ‘We have had some interest from production companies,’ says Cholmondeley coyly.

In the museum’s reconstructed listening quarters, the narrative shifts from the glittering world of Sir Philip Sassoon to the shadowy world of wartime espionage. The transition is striking: one moment I’m surrounded by the languid elegance of the 1920s and 1930s; the next I’m plunged into blackout Britain.

As we emerge onto the terrace, sunlight catches the newly restored brickwork. Beyond lies the landscape Sassoon once knew: the lake, the park and the wider estate that stretched across farms and woodland. Among them lies the old swimming pool, one of the earliest heated pools in the country. Did the Germans ever use it? ‘You know what – that’s one thing I don’t know,’ says Alan Perkin, another trustee, who has guided me through the basement, sharing his remarkable knowledge of its wartime operations. Nearby are the remains of tunnels through which the volunteers would come and go daily. Vast terraces descend towards the water, restoring something of the theatrical grandeur that guests would have experienced in the house’s social heyday.

What would Cholmondeley’s great-uncle have thought of the transformation? ‘I think he would have wanted the house to be used for the war,’ he says, simply. ‘He did so much to rearm during the ’20s and ’30s.’

Sassoon created a palace built for diversion and conversation among the great and the good; later it became a house where every word carried consequences. In the years following, Trent Park kept its secrets. Until now.
