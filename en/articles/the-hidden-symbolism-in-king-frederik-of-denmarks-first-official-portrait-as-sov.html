The hidden symbolism in King Frederik of Denmark's first official portrait as sovereign

The King was joined by his wife, Queen Mary of Denmark, for the unveiling of his first portrait, which includes nods to his mother, his decorated military history, and his whimsical nickname

By Isaac Bickerstaff · September 28, 2026

It was a momentous day for King Frederik of Denmark on Friday, as the monarch joined his wife, Queen Mary of Denmark, to unveil the first official portrait of his reign.

King Frederik, 58, looked characteristically dapper in black tie, while Queen Mary was a vision in white, wearing a fairy-tale gown by Australian designer Maticevski, adorned with gold stencilling. The royal couple received flowers and a bow from a young boy before entering Frederiksborg Castle’s great hall, which plays host to the royal portraits of monarchs from the House of Glücksberg, dating back to King Christian IX.

Joining their ranks on Friday was Cathrine Raben Davidsen’s painting of King Frederik. Described as one of Denmark’s leading visual artists, she was commissioned to create the monarch’s first official portrait two years ago, when he ascended the throne following the abdication of his mother, Queen Margrethe.

In a social media post showcasing the portrait, which depicts the King in the full regalia of the Royal Danish Navy, surrounded by a number of important personal and royal symbols, Frederiksborg Castle revealed some of the hidden meanings in the portrait, which is described as depicting ‘the man behind the King, with more gravity and gentleness in his gaze.’

The soft green background is a nod to King Frederik’s military past, taking inspiration from the Kongsøre Forest, where the monarch completed parts of his training with the Danish Frogman Corps, an elite section of naval special operations forces. The experience was a profound one for the King, then Crown Prince, earning him his enduring nickname, ‘Pingo’, after his wetsuit filled with water, forcing him to waddle like a penguin. King Frederik also has a penguin tattoo on his right bicep, inspired by the experience.

The background is also adorned with daisies, described by the Castle as a ‘tribute to Queen Margrethe,’ whose nickname – both from her family and from royals around the world – is ‘Daisy’. The former Danish queen is known to wear a daisy brooch in honour of the moniker. In another royal nod, the King is holding a sabre that once belonged to his mother’s father, King Frederik IX.

The Castle goes on to acknowledge the signature style of the artist, writing: ‘Tradition, history and deeply personal details come together in a portrait that is also unmistakably Cathrine Raben Davidsen: visible brushstrokes, translucent layers and a figure that almost merges with the surrounding landscape.’

This modern approach to royal portraiture was also adopted by Jonathan Yeo, who accepted a commission in 2020 to paint King Charles, then Prince of Wales, to celebrate his half-century tenure at The Drapers’ Company. Unveiled in 2024, the portrait became the first official portrait of the King following the Coronation.

‘I’m unbelievably grateful for the opportunity to capture such an extraordinary and unique person, especially at the historic moment of becoming King,’ said Yeo at the time, while detailing some of the metaphors hidden within the work.

‘When I started this project, His Majesty The King was still His Royal Highness The Prince of Wales, and much like the butterfly I've painted hovering over his shoulder, this portrait has evolved as the subject's role in our public life has transformed.’

In his description of the portrait, Yeo elaborates how the butterfly, which is often, he said, a ‘symbol of metamorphosis and rebirth,’ is also intended to provide ‘a visual contrast to the military steeliness of the uniform and sword.’

The monarch butterfly is also suggestive of King Charles III’s environmental activism, being one of the migratory species most affected by climate change. The focus of his work, he argued, should always be the humanity of the subject. Indeed, it is characteristic of Yeo’s style that the Welsh Guard uniform blurs into the bright red brushstrokes of the portrait’s background, foregrounding the King’s expression – much like Davidsen’s painting of King Frederik

‘As a portrait artist, you get this unique opportunity to spend time with and get to know a subject,’ says the artist of his four sittings with Charles, which took place between Highgrove and Clarence House.

‘I wanted to minimise the visual distractions and allow people to connect with the human being underneath.’
