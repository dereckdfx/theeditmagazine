Why Jean Schlumberger might be the most important name in jewellery you've never heard of

At Tiffany &Co., French jewellery designer Jean Schlumberger created some of the house's most enduring silhouettes, loved by society swans including Jackie Kennedy and Bunny Mellon. Now, his artistic vision is being conserved for future generations thanks to a new partnership with Paris' Musée des Arts Décoratifs

By Chandler Tregaskes · October 6, 2026

By the time Jean Schlumberger had joined Tiffany & Co. in 1956, he had already cut his teeth creating surrealist buttons for Elsa Schiaparelli in Paris; designed womenswear for the New York label Chez Ninon (the American couturier who would later famously make the Chanel-authorised recreation of the French Maison’s classic pink tweed twin suit that Jackie Kennedy would wear on the day of her husband’s assassination); and served as an army officer in the French military during the Second World War, which took him to the Battle of Dunkirk, as well as stints in Syria and Lebanon with the Free French Forces.

From Prince William's confirmation to Princess Eugenie's wedding and Queen Elizabeth II's first Christmas without Prince Philip, the White Drawing Room at Windsor Castle has been at the centre of generations of royal events

Following the war, he successfully opened his own jewellery salon with business partner Nicolas Bongard and amassed a lofty clientele of society swans who adored his sculptural silhouettes. His motifs rejected the notions of clean-cut symmetry popular at the time and instead took cues from ephemeral, living flora and fauna, garnering himself a reputation as a visionary of the era. ‘Johnny’, as Diana Vreeland affectionately called him, once noted that he ‘appreciates the miracle of jewels. For him, they are the ways and means to the realization of his dreams.’

Then, Tiffany came calling. Or, specifically, Chairman Walter Hoving. During his over 20-year tenure at its design helm, he would go on to craft titular Tiffany & Co. creations that punctuated the world of high jewellery during the mid-to-late 20th century, and live on in sumptuous style legacy to this day. Think of 1965’s Bird on a Rock Brooch, which just got a reimagining by present-day senior vice president, Nathalie Verdeille, as recently as September; or 1961’s Ribbon Rosette Necklace, which was worn by Audrey Hepburn to promote Breakfast at Tiffany’s that year and has since inspired countless bow-adorned silhouettes from the house. Ever the jewellery architect, his designs masterfully blended real-life riffs with other-worldly wonder; immense modern craftsmanship with timeless elegance.

For Schlumberger, though, his fantastical visions always started on the page. In fact, as a child, he sketched so often that his parents would attempt to discourage his artistic tendencies, wanting him to opt for a more traditional career path. However – and luckily for the likes of the Duchess of Windsor, Babe Paley, Mona von Bismarck, Gloria Guinness and Princess Marina of Greece and Denmark, who would become among his most treasured clientele – this early effort to steer him away from his artistic endeavours never solidified. By the 1960s, the international elite of esteemed elegance had firmly found their artiste of choice. Jackie Kennedy would wear so many of his wrist adornments that the press would eventually dub them his ‘Jackie bracelets’.

Now, his original sketches - the blueprints of his most indelible objet - have become the protagonist in their own story: a new conservation project with Musée des Arts Décoratif to help preserve his legacy for the future. In a statement from the jewellery house, Tiffany & Co. confirmed, ‘Through a multi-year initiative led by The Tiffany Archives, the partners are conserving and digitally imaging approximately 5,000 original drawings and 5,000 archival documents, ensuring that these extraordinary works are preserved for future generations of scholars, curators and designers.’

As part of the project, a presentation during Paris Fashion Week displayed dubbed I Am Only a Pencil offered guests an insight into the mind of Schlumberger, and an entrée into the years-long preservation initiative in partnership with the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts.

It feels rather apt for the master jeweller who once claimed, ‘My designs are what matter; I don't make jewellery.’ As part of the hand-drawn artefacts being protected and upheld, a number of pieces from long-time supporter, American socialite, horticulturist, art collector and style prophet, Rachel Mellon, remain at the core of the project.

‘Bunny,’ as she was known to friends, was the granddaughter of Listerine inventor Jordan Lambert, daughter of Gillette president and Warner-Lambert founder Gerard Barnes Lambert, and wife of art collector and thoroughbred breeder, Paul Mellon (she was also previously married to fellow horsebreeder and businessman, Stacy Barcroft Lloyd Junior). She was, in many regards, the epitome of upper echelon Stateside grandeur. Her close friendship with Jackie Kennedy led to her helping to decorate the First Lady’s Georgetown house where she first met the to-be President; the White House Rose Garden; and restoring the presidential home itself. ‘I loved your house, but I don’t like mine,’ Kennedy once confessed to Mellon over the phone, flattering her more so by commenting on ‘even [loving] the stale candy in the antique jars’. This, according to Mellon, is how she ‘won me over’.

When it came to Schlumberger, Mellon’s sophisticated tastes had found nirvana. In fact, one of the designer’s earliest ‘Bird on a Rock’ brooches belonged to Bunny, hers perched on a blue lapis lazuli in 1965. This piece found itself as part of the recent presentation, along with seven other jewels - and their sketches - from her collection, like a pair of ornate butterfly bracelets from 1956 which feature diamonds, amethysts, sapphires, turquoises, peridots on gold and platinum - as well as their coordinating collier; a number of ‘Losange and Dot’ bangles from the 1960s; and the gold, platinum, turquoise and diamond ‘Leaves Necklace, also from 1956.

From wreaths of golden tulips wrapped around the neck, to mermaid candelabras and religious orbs encased in gilded crucifixes; Schlumberger’s era-defining eye is the stuff of fashion legend. Now his style saga is set to endure forevermore…
