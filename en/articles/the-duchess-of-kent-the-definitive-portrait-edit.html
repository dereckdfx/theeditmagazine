Royals

The Duchess of Kent: the definitive portrait of the royal who married Queen Elizabeth II's cousin and left the spotlight to become a teacher

The Duchess of Kent married the Duke of Kent and dutifully served the crown – before becoming a school teacher. Hugo Vickers went inside palace circles to paint the definitive portrait of an extraordinary royal, who died one year ago today

By Hugo Vickers · September 4, 2026

There was an extraordinary response to the death of the Duchess of Kent in September. That she hadn’t been seen in public for many years might have meant she was forgotten, but as I found out when working on my book A Royal Life with the Duke of Kent, she retained a great number of fans. When someone dies, their life is re-told in the round – and the conclusion was that as a member of the Royal Family she got it absolutely right (at a time when one or two had notably got it wrong). She undertook her duties as a member of the Royal Family for about 40 years, with no wish for self-aggrandisement, never to take, only to give. She used her natural gifts to help others. She was beautiful, her features captured many times by Cecil Beaton, Barry Lategan, Norman Parkinson and Mario Testino, and in a portrait by Pietro Annigoni. She dressed stylishly but more importantly added a compassionate touch to much that she did, perhaps best remembered when she consoled the Czech player Jana Novotná when she lost at Wimbledon in 1993.

Long before I met her, I had an abiding memory of seeing her at the State Opening of Parliament on 21 April 1966, coincidentally the 40th birthday of Queen Elizabeth II. In those days, the Chamber of the House of Lords was filled with peers in parliamentary robes, and peeresses in evening dress and glittering tiaras, with well-known figures from the war taking part: Lord Montgomery of Alamein, holding the Sword of State; Lord Mountbatten as Gold Stick. The Queen was supported by her family. Seven of them processed into the Chamber before the Queen’s procession. The royal ladies were all stars in their own right – Princess Margaret, the Duchess of Gloucester, Princess Marina and, in pale blue with her hair swept back, the Duchess of Kent. Like for State Banquets today, the royal ladies were glamorous and elegantly dressed, often adorned with fine jewellery, tiaras, orders and decorations.

Her death on 4 September reminded us of much of this and hit a particular chord. Her funeral at Westminster Cathedral was the first requiem mass for a member of the Royal Family. This was a quite extraordinarily uplifting and unifying occasion, blending the traditional elements of a Roman Catholic service with those of the Royal Family and the Church of England. In a small but significant way, it nudged the ecumenical movement forward. It was the first time the King had attended such a service in Britain, and it opened with a message from Pope Leo XIV, delivered primarily to the King and later to the Duke of Kent and his family by the Apostolic Nuncio to Great Britain, Archbishop Miguel Maury Buendía – a rare message in which he recognised the duchess’s ‘legacy of Christian goodness’ and imparted his ‘apostolic blessing as a pledge of consolation and peace in the risen Lord’. At the end, the Pipe Major Andrew Rayment of the Royal Dragoon Guards played the lament Sleep, Dearie, Sleep, and there were two verses of the national anthem. After the fine singing and the incense of the cathedral, the immediate family travelled with the coffin to Windsor. After a short break at Frogmore House, they went to the Royal Burial Ground, essentially a garden in the shadow of Queen Victoria’s mausoleum where, since 1928, most members of the Royal Family have been buried and where, when at Windsor, the late Queen often walked, knowing as she did so many who lay under the flat stones.

There the Dean of Windsor presided and the duchess was laid to rest near Prince George and Princess Marina, and Sir Angus Ogilvy (husband of Princess Alexandra). Her youngest grandson, Louis Windsor, was proud to hold the bowl with the earth to scatter on the coffin.

The epitome of casual chic in a khaki coat, Lady Helen Taylor did her bit for a charity close to William and Catherine's hearts

The Duchess of Kent was born Katharine Worsley in Yorkshire in 1933. She adored her father, Sir William Worsley, 4th Baronet, Lord Lieutenant of the North Riding, and a keen cricketer. Her nephew, also Sir William, recalled: ‘She was the youngest and the only girl in the family and very close to her father. It was a wonderful father-daughter relationship. Hovingham gave her roots, which proved useful in her later life in the Royal Family. The duke’s father had died when he was young, so my grandfather was a kind of surrogate father to him. They could come to Hovingham informally for picnics in the woods. My grandfather had a 12-seater Land Rover with a cooker in the back. Having been a prisoner in the First World War, he liked cooking sausages. It was all very informal.’ The duke’s affection for his father-in-law was recorded in an anonymous tribute in The Times after he died in 1973. He wrote of his ‘devastating and unique sense of humour, which flattened pomp and pretence irresistibly’. He recalled a Hovingham pensioner saying: ‘He was like a father to us all.’ Other neighbours remembered Katharine happily swilling out the pigs, and when she learnt that children in a hospital in Kirkbymoorside had missed seeing her wedding at York Minster in 1961, she sent them all a piece of her wedding cake.

Yorkshire remained important to the Duchess of Kent throughout her life. Sir William recalled that after the serious flooding in 2015, in which part of Tadcaster Bridge was washed away, his aunt rang him and said she thought Yorkshire was feeling neglected and needed a visit. She came in January 2016: ‘I can’t tell you how professional she was, and everyone was thrilled that she came.She hadn’t done royal duties for many years, but she slipped back into the role.’

Lady Helen Taylor donned her mother's sapphire-and-diamond engagement ring at her intimate family vigil and funeral

The Duchess of Kent was educated at Queen Margaret’s School, which was housed at Castle Howard during the war. She used to ride over there every day on her pony. In London, she taught at the fashionable girls’ school Lady Eden’s in Kensington, which was founded by Sir Anthony Eden’s sister-in-law. Here, she gained useful skills. When Katharine married the Duke of Kent in 1961, she soon became one of the most popular members of the Royal Family, the subject of many beautiful photographic portraits and a stylish and original dresser. Her outgoing personality was a huge help to the duke, who was more naturally reserved. One of her close friends said: ‘She was a perfect member of the Royal Family. She never had any side to her. There was not an ounce of self-importance. She was genuinely humble. And she had a great sense of humour. She was great fun.’

The most successful members of the Royal Family are those who support the monarch rather than competing with them – the Kents got that right from the start. In the early days of their marriage, they were busy supporting the Queen in numerous ways. They undertook engagements not only in Britain, but as far afield as Iran, Uganda, the Gambia, Barbados and Japan, attending Independence ceremonies and the coronation of the king of Tonga. At Wren House, their home at Kensington Palace, the duchess told me that she considered it a great privilege to have been able to visit so many countries, to meet so many interesting people in all kinds of walks of life, to talk to them and learn about their lives. Sometimes the duke was stationed abroad, and she went with him. Above all, they loved their year in Hong Kong, when their son George was a little boy.

The oldest working member of the royal family, the Duke of Kent has endured grief and increasingly frail health this year

The Kents began married life at Coppins in Iver, near Windsor, the home Prince George inherited from his aunt, Princess Victoria, where, as a young widow on her own, Princess Marina had brought up the duke, Princess Alexandra and Prince Michael. Later, they moved to Anmer Hall in Norfolk, which again they loved.

The duchess’s daughter, Lady Helen Taylor, said that in those days the foreign travel was long and her mother found it difficult being away from her children for occasional extensive periods of time. She was sensitive to the suffering of others. If she went to Helen House hospice and sat with a dying child, it took a lot out of her. She would drive Helen to school at PNEU in Datchet from Coppins. One time, a school friend inadvertently shut the duchess’s finger in the car door. ‘She was playing the piano a lot then, and so had to have the tip of her finger sewn back on,’ said Helen. She also remembered how her mother loved clothes, which gave her confidence when doing her public duties: ‘At the end of each season, she sold all her clothes to finance buying new ones.’

In 1994, the duchess asked the Queen’s permission to convert to Roman Catholicism. She had terminated a pregnancy on medical advice and then suffered a miscarriage in 1977. The unhappiness that followed this caused her to reflect on her faith. She undertook a pilgrimage to Walsingham with Robert Runcie, then Archbishop of Canterbury. In due course, this led her to talks with Bishop Gordon Wheeler of Leeds, and in turn to Cardinal Basil Hume, Archbishop of Westminster. He had been Abbot of Ampleforth, which was near her childhood home in Hovingham. He admitted her into the Catholic Church in 1994.

She trained as a Samaritan; she worked as a volunteer for The Passage, which supports the homeless in London; she went on pilgrimages to Lourdes, happy to roll up her sleeves, all done with none of the public attention of her earlier royal life. A close friend said: ‘She was very spiritual. She loved just being a normal person. She used to chat away to everyone. They never knew who she was. She had a wonderful sense of humour and was very kind.’ It would be wrong to suggest she was reclusive. She just worked out of the public eye. About 25 years ago, the agent Sue Ayton helped her arrange occasional interviews and talks. A notable engagement covered by ITN was when she visited a home for orphans in Macedonia in 1999

Music had always played a large part in her life. Her daughter said that her mother’s piano playing was almost of professional concert standard. The duchess asked the Queen if she could relinquish her traditional royal duties, stepping down from being chancellor of Leeds University and ceasing formal connection with her regiments and charities. Her Majesty agreed. Most privately, the duchess went to Hull and gave singing lessons once a week at Wansbeck Primary School. There will be many children in Hull (all grown up today) who will only now discover that the ‘Mrs Kent’ who taught them to sing or play the piano had had a whole other life before she met them to teach.

In 2003, Nicholas Robinson, the headmaster of King’s College School in Cambridge (where her two granddaughters were pupils), invited her to open a new library and classroom. She told him that she did not do that kind of engagement, so he suggested a more informal visit. This led to a discussion about the lack of musical opportunities in Hull. From this, the charity Future Talent was founded. Its mission is to provide music support and masterclasses, run ensemble days and workshops, and contribute to the cost of musical instruments for underprivileged children between the ages of six and 18. The duchess told me: ‘I realised that so many children from certain areas of the inner cities were never going to get music teaching… We start them young – the younger the better. We can get music into their bodies and then it just flows.’ Future Talent now supports 150 musical children all over the UK and is forever expanding. Robinson hailed her as one of the few ‘senior members of the Royal Family to have set up her own charity’. The last time the duchess attended a formal event was a concert in St James’s Palace in the summer of 2023, at which young musicians performed. If she had been nervous at attending, she relaxed the moment she saw the young musicians.

Robinson enjoyed a warm friendship with her for 21 years. Her eyes would light up when she saw him and she would be keen to know how things were going. He remembered that former pupils told him how encouraging she had been: ‘Josh Batty, now the youngest principal flautist with the Sydney Symphony Orchestra in Australia, recalled how the duchess used to ring him to find out how he was getting on. Adam O’Shea, another alumnus, a pianist and singer, had the same experience.’ Future Talent was ‘her legacy – a vision to support young musicians’, said Robinson.

The duchess spent her last years at Wren House, which she shared with the duke. One night in March 2013, he said goodnight to her and couldn’t lift his arm. She realised he had had a stroke – luckily, a mild one. She called an ambulance, and he was treated in time.

I first met her after a concert at Marlborough House and, in telling her about Commonwealth Walkways, hope I did not bore her too much by extolling the benefits of dropped kerbs forpartially sighted people. When I was working on A Royal Life with the Duke of Kent, she said there could not be a book about him without her taking part. I went to see her and we looked at cine films of their earlier life at Coppins, holidays, safaris, and waterskiing while on honeymoon. Besides that interview, it was fun to go to tea a few times on a Saturday afternoon, where conversation ranged over many topics, including (I’m not quite sure how) Cecil Beaton’s love affair with Greta Garbo.

After the duchess died, I posted a photograph on Instagram of the duke taking her to the hairdresser, snapped by a random photographer. This was viewed by 2.3 million people, and 36,000 of them pressed the like button. It caught a touching moment – the devotion in a marriage of 64 years. In the last few years, her health had been frail, but the duke took good care of her, never leaving her for long periods, and even though she had a carer, he cut short his last holiday in Norfolk to be with her.

A perfect tribute would be a donation to futuretalent.org – a gift in her name to the children whose lives are changed by music.
