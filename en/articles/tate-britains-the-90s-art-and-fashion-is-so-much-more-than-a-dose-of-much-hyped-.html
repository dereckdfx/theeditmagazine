Tate Britain's ‘The 90s: Art and Fashion’ is so much more than a dose of much-hyped nostalgia

Forget Calvin Klein minimalism and Britpop nostalgia, Edward Enninful's highly anticipated exhibition reveals a decade more messy and meaningful than we remember

By Isolde Walters · October 6, 2026

Tate Britain’s ‘The 90s: Art and Fashion’ exhibition starts as it means to go on. Visitors to the much-hyped show are greeted by a blown-up print of the famous Steven Klein shot of Kate Moss and Naomi Campbell sitting on a stoop, cigarettes in hand, dripping with insouciant cool. Nearby, on a bank of reassuringly solid televisions, Pulp’s ‘Common People’ and Sinéad O'Connor’s ‘Nothing Compares 2 U’ play. On the opposite wall hangs Nigel Shafran’s photograph of the then-most famous feet in the world: a pair of scruffy trainers, some sexy mules, and three pairs of stonkingly chunky platform clompers. Can you guess who these feet belong to? The Spice Girls, of course.

Welcome to the ’90s, so often looked back on as nirvana, before the internet and social media arrived to ruin the party, when there was money sloshing around and freedom too, a gloriously buoyant time, where optimism and hedonism abounded in equal measure. Surely the true test of this exhibition, curated by Edward Enninful OBE – who was, as fashion director of i-D magazine, at the beating heart of the sartorial scene – is whether it says anything new about this much-mythologised decade.

Once a glittering society retreat and later the scene of audacious wartime espionage, Trent Park is ready for its big reveal. The Marquess of Cholmondeley gives Edit a tour

And yes, it does. This exhibition reveals the ’90s to be a much grittier, darker, richer decade than the clean and chic, Calvin-Klein-meets-Carolyn-Bessette-Kennedy-style minimalism it is increasingly reduced to. In the first room, we encounter the street photography that took off in those unsettled early years, after Margaret Thatcher resigned and Britain slid into recession. Gone is the glamour, opulence and artifice of the ’80s, and instead here is Kate Moss, shot by Corinne Day, in those famously flimsy knickers, a string of fairy lights serving as a kind of halo, in the image that to many would come to define ‘heroin chic’.

The picture is, to my mind, breathtakingly beautiful. There are other images in the room, though, that show the uglier grittiness of Day’s photography: Rosemary Ferguson sprawled on a sofa in shiny leather trousers, the room around her downright depressing, the carpet grubby with what looks like cigarette paraphernalia (and who knows what else) and empty cans. Juergen Teller’s snapshots – including the famous one of La Moss with pink hair, bare face and a grin – showcase a defiant new aesthetic. There’s a young Gisele Bundchen, as we rarely see her, stripped of the glamour, perched in white jeans, an unzipped hoodie, and Birkenstocks, on the hood of a Mercedes in what looks like the streets of Primrose Hill.

The next room, dedicated to nightlife, is titled ‘I can’t get no sleep’, from a lyric in the Faithless song ‘Insomnia’. It’s difficult not to walk around this room and wish we could bring these times back. Here are revellers without phones, snapped in moments of carefree abandon, in line outside the newly established Ministry of Sound, or truly anonymous as they clamber over a box at the queer night Kinky Gerlinky.

Body Shock is a room dominated by the designs of Alexander McQueen. Here we see Joan of Arc’s burning skin transformed into a scarlet beaded gown with matching headpiece, as well as his exquisite body armour and a sharply tailored jacket with horns protruding from the shoulders. The darkness of these designs is mirrored by the gothic, provocative works by Helen Chadwick, Jake and Dinos Chapman and Cathy de Monchaux, also on display. It is impossible not to be struck by the tragic end of both McQueen and his muse Isabella Blow, whose portrait hangs on the wall and who served as Edit’s fashion editor; both died by suicide.

The exhibition serves up the hits you’d expect, especially from a curator so ultra-connected in the fashion world. There are the famous purple crocodile-effect platforms that Naomi Campbell took a tumble from at a Vivienne Westwood show. The John Galliano crinoline gown that Kate Moss memorably ran down the runway in, as the designer had instructed her, as though she were fleeing a pack of wolves. There’s a naughty neon sculpture by Tracey Emin; a playful wallpapered wall by Abigail Lane of a repeated print of a female bottom, as Lane puts it, ‘a kind of artistic mooning’; as well as works by Damien Hirst, Sam Taylor-Johnson and Sarah Lucas.

But the piece that stopped me in my tracks, I had never even heard of before heading into the exhibition. It hangs in the ‘Nation’s Finest’ room, dedicated to works by Black British artists, photographers and designers who were excluded from the overwhelmingly white image of ‘Cool Britannia’. It is a portrait of Doreen Lawrence, the mother of Stephen Lawrence, who was murdered in a racially motivated attack that caused outcry in 1993, and led, eventually, to the Macpherson inquiry and historic police reforms. In this portrait, titled ‘No Woman, No Cry’, painted by Chris Ofili in 1998, tears cascade down Doreen’s cheeks and the teardrops are filled with collaged images of her son’s face. It’s a startling and deeply moving artwork, bringing home to the viewer that there was more to this heralded decade than nightlife, Stella McCartney frocks and pretty pictures of Kate Moss.

The '90s were messier and more meaningful than our nostalgia allows. Go to Tate Britain and see for yourself.

‘The 90s: Art and Fashion’ will run from 8 October 2026 to 14 February 2027 at Tate Britain. Tickets are available at tate.org.uk
