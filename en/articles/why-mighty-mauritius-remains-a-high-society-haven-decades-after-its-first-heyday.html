Why mighty Mauritius remains a high-society haven, decades after the first jet-set boom

Mauritius, which attracted the jet-set in their droves during its golden-era heyday, remains an emerald-green, Indian Ocean idyll for travellers seeking a dose of winter sun

By Stephanie Bridger-Linning · September 18, 2026

My husband and I honeymooned in Mauritius shortly after lockdown, drawn to the promise of an Indian Ocean idyll with emerald-green beauty to rival the Maldives but with a little more adventure. The two-week visit was spent hiking through the verdant valleys and beneath the thundering waterfalls of the Black River Gorges National Park in the centre of the island before retreating to the talcum-white shores of the south-west to do – well – not very much.

Five years later and it was time to return, only this time with a toddler in tow. Alas, fewer hikes, fewer waterfalls, and certainly fewer hours aimlessly spent flicking through books on the beach but, fortunately, no less luxurious. This was thanks to the Shangri-La Le Touessrok, perched on the east coast, where each room and suite boasts a few across the vivid cerulean water.

Bid goodbye to yacht parties and island hopping and book a room in one of these retreats where silence is golden and quiet glamour prevails

Tracing its history back to 1978, the hotel retains a sense of the golden-era glamour of this island’s initial tourism boom: international sun-seekers came in their droves to unwind at the new luxury hotels, basking in the heat 20 degrees south of the equator. Society was smitten. By the 1990s, the north-coast village of Grand Baie was considered the St Tropez of Mauritius, attracting an appropriately starry crowd that included a young Prince William (on an ‘educational’ gap year) as well as Princess Stephanie of Monaco. It remains popular with the jet set: Zimbabwean jewellery designer Chelsy Davy (famous as the ex-girlfriend of Prince Harry) and her husband, Sam Cutmore-Scott, live on the island for part of the year, following in the footsteps of her parents.

The Shangri-La Le Touessrok offers five restaurants (including Kushi, a stylish Japanese restaurant overlooking the lagoon) and six beaches, where butlers – summoned with the press of a button – keep guests refreshed with iced water and slices of fresh fruit that materialise at just the right moment.

The facilities for families are excellent: children are given their own thoughtful welcome hamper on arrival, the kids’ club provides all-day childcare, and restaurants are well set-up to accommodate the whims and fancies of temperamental toddlers – and teens. Children are even welcome at Chi, The Spa, where a ‘mommy and me’ joint treatment offers the most relaxing way to spend quality time together.

But honeymooners seeking a romantic escape needn’t be put off: there is an adults-only pool, cocooned cabanas and high-energy, high-jinks at Sega Bar, the late-night poolside venue where guests sip cocktails crafted with local rum.

The highlight of any stay, however, has to be Ilot Mangénie: a rugged, windswept island accessed by complimentary boat shuttles from the main hotel. Although not technically private (no beaches on Mauritius are), only Shangri-La guests can live their Robinson Crusoe fantasies with barefoot lunches of artisan pizzas, fresh salads and seafood platters, followed by a relaxed afternoon sprawled out on the double-width loungers, gazing out to the whipped-up waves.

At the end of the day it is time to return home to the fresh, bright, white-washed sleekness of the room (ours, in the Frangipani wing, offered uninterrupted views over the sea). Yes, this visit to the island was very different to our first – but this grande dame of the island’s hotel scene meant it was no less memorable.
